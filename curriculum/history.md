# History of Civilization — the syllabus

One chapter per day, in this order, from the first tools to the present. The
loop writes chapter N+1 where N is the number of files already in
`lessons/history/`. Entries are numbered continuously across parts; each line
is `N. **Title** — scope`.

The lens: how humans captured **energy**, built **tools**, stored and moved
**information**, organized **money and trade**, and projected **force**, and
how each of those shaped the societies that followed. Less detail than Durant's
*Story of Civilization*, more technology than a textbook. Names appear when a
person really mattered (Hammurabi, Archimedes, Gutenberg, Watt) and are flagged
in `names` so they can be remembered, but the thread is always the capability,
not the biography. When fewer than 30 entries remain unwritten, append the
next part in the same style.

## Part I — Before civilization (c. 3,000,000–10,000 BCE)

1. **Why study the whole arc** — the five threads of this series (energy, tools, information, money, force) and the rule that technology compounds while human nature doesn't
2. **The first tools** — Oldowan choppers and Acheulean hand-axes; a million years of one design
3. **Fire** — cooked food, the bigger brain, the longer day, and the first energy technology
4. **Language** — the information technology that made cumulative culture possible
5. **The hunter-gatherer economy** — band size, Dunbar's number, egalitarianism, mobility, and why wealth couldn't accumulate
6. **Out of Africa** — migration, the peopling of the continents, and the Neanderthal encounter
7. **The bow, the needle, the boat** — the Upper Paleolithic toolkit and the first long-distance trade (obsidian, shells)
8. **The dog and the first domestication** — the beginning of humans engineering other species
9. **Cave art and counting** — symbolic thought, tally sticks (Ishango bone), and the roots of record-keeping

## Part II — The agricultural revolution (c. 10,000–3500 BCE)

10. **The end of the Ice Age and the Fertile Crescent** — climate, wild grasses, and the Natufians

12. **Domesticating animals** — sheep, goats, cattle, pigs; milk, wool, traction, and disease
13. **Göbekli Tepe and the temple before the town** — monuments before agriculture?
14. **Çatalhöyük and Jericho** — the first large settlements and what they tell us about early inequality
15. **Pottery** — storage, cooking, and the first mass-produced artifact
16. **The plough and irrigation** — energy from animals and rivers; surplus, specialization, and the birth of the state
17. **Property, surplus, and hierarchy** — grain as taxable wealth (Scott's "Against the Grain"); why farming made life worse before it made it better
18. **Weaving and textiles** — spinning, looms, and the hidden half of ancient labor
19. **The wheel** — potter's wheel to cart; why the Americas didn't use it
20. **Copper and the first metallurgy** — Ötzi's axe and the Chalcolithic
