Mental models, one at a time

Mental Models

A latticework of the big ideas from the big disciplines, built one model at a time in the spirit of Charlie Munger. Read in order or dip in by discipline.

Part I — Tools for thinking

  1. No. 1 ✓
    The latticework Why isolated facts don't stick, and what to build instead

    Charlie Munger's central idea: knowledge becomes usable only when the big models from many disciplines are hung on one frame and checked against each other. Someone holding a single model forces every problem into it, and someone holding eighty can see which model fits the problem in front of them.

    03 Oct
  2. No. 2 ✓
    The map is not the territory All models are wrong, some are useful, and the skill is knowing which part is wrong today

    Every description of reality leaves things out, and that is what makes it usable. Trouble starts when the user forgets which parts were left out, or who did the leaving. The lesson is about reading maps as maps: trusting them for what they were drawn to show, and checking the ground when the stakes are high.

    04 Oct
  3. No. 3
    Circle of competence Knowing the edge of what you know, and what to do when you reach it

    Everyone has a region in which their judgment is reliable and a much larger region in which it only feels reliable. The model does not ask for a big circle; it asks for an honest line around it and different behaviour on each side. Most expensive mistakes are made a few steps outside the line by people who did not notice crossing it.

    05 Oct
  4. No. 4
    Inversion Invert, always invert: solve problems backward, and avoid stupidity before you seek brilliance

    Many problems that are hard to solve forward are easy to solve backward. Instead of asking how to reach a goal, ask what would guarantee failure, and then arrange for none of those things to happen. The model comes from mathematics, was made a rule of life by Charlie Munger, and works because minds generate failure modes far more reliably than they generate winning strategies.

    06 Oct
  5. No. 5
    First-principles reasoning Reason from what you know to be true, not from what everyone else does, and know when borrowing is the smarter move

    First-principles reasoning means taking a problem apart until it rests on facts known to be true on their own, then building the answer up from those, instead of copying what similar things cost or how similar things are done. It is the tool that finds ten-fold improvements, and it is expensive, so most of the skill lies in knowing when analogy is the better bet.

    08 Oct
  6. No. 6
    Second-order thinking And then what? Following consequences past the first step, and knowing when to stop

    Second-order thinking is the habit of asking what the effects of a decision will cause in turn, after the people and systems affected have responded. First-order thinking stops at the obvious result. Second-order thinking follows the chain one or two steps further, and it is the difference between a policy that works on paper and one that works on people.

    12 Oct
  7. No. 7
    Probabilistic thinking Thinking in bets, base rates, and the difference between a good decision and a good outcome

    Probabilistic thinking is the habit of holding beliefs as degrees of confidence and of choosing by weighing what could happen against how likely each outcome is. It separates the quality of a decision from the quality of its result, because the two come apart whenever chance is involved. The tool is a number, even a rough one, attached to every claim that matters.

    14 Oct
  8. No. 8
    Bayesian updating Priors, evidence, and changing your mind by the right amount

    Bayesian updating is the rule for moving a belief when a new fact arrives: start from how likely the claim was before, ask how much more likely the fact is if the claim is true than if it is false, and multiply. The middle quantity, how sharply the evidence discriminates, is the one people skip, and most bad updates come from skipping it.

    16 Oct
  9. No. 9
    Expected value Magnitude times probability; why rare, large outcomes dominate

    Expected value is the average result of a decision if it could be run many times: each outcome's payoff weighted by its probability, then added up. It is the tool that makes small chances of large results legible, and it is most often misused where the bet cannot be repeated or the worst branch cannot be survived.

    18 Oct
