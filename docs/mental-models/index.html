Mental Models

A latticework of the big ideas from the big disciplines, built one model at a time in the spirit of Charlie Munger. Read in order or dip in by discipline.

Part I — Tools for thinking

  1. No. 1 ✓
    The latticework Why isolated facts don't stick, and what to build instead

    Charlie Munger's central idea: knowledge only becomes usable when the big models from many disciplines are hung on a single mental frame and checked against each other. A person with one model forces every problem into it. A person with eighty sees which model actually fits.

    03 Oct
  2. No. 2 ✓
    The map is not the territory All models are wrong, some are useful, and the skill is knowing which part is wrong today

    Every description of reality leaves things out; that is what makes it usable. Trouble starts when you forget which parts were left out, or who did the leaving. This lesson is about reading maps as maps: trusting them for what they were drawn to show, and checking the ground when the stakes are high.

    04 Oct
  3. No. 3
    Circle of competence Knowing the edge of what you know, and what to do when you reach it

    Everyone has a region where their judgment is reliable and a much larger region where it only feels reliable. The model is not to make the circle big; it is to know where its boundary runs, and to behave differently on each side of it. Most expensive mistakes are made a few steps outside the line by people who did not notice crossing it.

    05 Oct
  4. No. 4
    Inversion Invert, always invert: solve problems backward, and avoid stupidity before you seek brilliance

    Many problems that are hard to solve forward are easy to solve backward. Instead of asking how to reach a goal, ask what would guarantee failure, and then arrange not to do those things. The model comes from mathematics, was popularized by Charlie Munger, and works because our minds generate failure modes far more reliably than they generate winning strategies.

    06 Oct
