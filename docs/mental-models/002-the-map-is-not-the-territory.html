The map is not the territory

All models are wrong, some are useful, and the skill is knowing which part is wrong today

✓ read · 4 October 2026 · that day's edition · all mental models

Every description of reality leaves things out; that is what makes it usable. Trouble starts when you forget which parts were left out, or who did the leaving. This lesson is about reading maps as maps: trusting them for what they were drawn to show, and checking the ground when the stakes are high.

A map is a model of a place that is useful precisely because it is not the place. It drops almost everything: the smell, the traffic, the slope of the road, the state of the bridge this morning. What is left is a small, structured set of claims that fit in your pocket. The model works as long as the structure it keeps matches the structure of the territory in the ways that matter for your trip. It fails, sometimes badly, when you forget that it was ever a reduction at all.

The phrase comes from Alfred Korzybski, a Polish-American scholar who wrote in 1931 that a map is not the territory it represents, but if it is correct it has a similar structure to the territory, and that similarity is what makes it useful. Korzybski was not talking about paper maps. He meant every word, number, chart, and theory we use to stand in for the world. The statistician George Box later compressed the same idea into the line that all models are wrong, but some are useful. Both halves of that sentence are the model.

Three maps that looked like the territory

Take a software dashboard. Every service shows green: CPU normal, error rate low, latency within bounds. Meanwhile users cannot log in, because a certificate expired on a system that the dashboard never measured. The dashboard was not lying. It was a map drawn to show certain things, and the failure happened in the blank space. Teams that have lived through this learn to ask what the dashboard cannot see, which is a different question from whether it is accurate.

Take a set of financial statements. Reported earnings are a map of a business drawn by accountants under rules written for other purposes. Enron's income statements were, for years, largely compliant with the rules and almost entirely detached from the cash the business generated. An investor who read the earnings as the business was reading a map that the mapmaker had drawn to be read that way. The investors who got out early were the ones who went and looked at cash flow, debt tucked into partnerships, and what the company actually sold.

Take a risk model. Before 2008, banks measured their exposure with models that assumed price changes followed a bell curve. The map was elegant and, most days, right. The territory contained fat tails: moves that the model said should happen once in thousands of years happened several times in a week. The models had been drawn in calm weather and were trusted in a storm. The lesson is not that models of risk are useless. It is that a model carries the assumptions of the period in which it was built, and those assumptions are the first thing to check when conditions change.

The questions that keep a map honest

Three questions turn a map back into a map. What was this drawn to show? A dashboard built for capacity planning will miss security. An earnings figure built for tax comparability will miss cash. What did it leave out, and could that matter here? The answer is often obvious once asked and invisible until then. Who drew it, and what did they want you to see? Most maps are drawn by someone with an interest in your route. That does not make them wrong, but it tells you where to be careful.

A good habit is to keep a short list of the maps you rely on daily and, for each, the one thing you know they cannot show. The error budget hides retries. The monthly report hides the week. The benchmark hides the workload that is not in the benchmark. The list is short and almost never changes, which is why it is worth writing down.

Where the model misleads

The model fails in two opposite directions. The first is the one everybody warns about: treating the map as the ground. The second is less discussed and just as costly. Having learned that all maps are wrong, a person refuses to use any of them, or demands a map so detailed that it is no longer a map. Borges wrote a one-paragraph story about an empire whose cartographers drew a map at a scale of one to one; it was useless and was left to rot in the desert. Someone who insists on seeing everything before deciding anything has built that map. The practical skill is not to distrust models. It is to use the cheap, wrong model for the ninety cases where its errors do not matter and to go and look at the ground in the ten cases where they do.

You can tell when someone is over-applying the idea. They say 'well, that is just a model' as a way of ending an argument rather than starting one. The proper response to 'the map is wrong' is 'wrong where, by how much, and does it matter for this trip?' A map that is wrong about the elevation of a hill you are not climbing is a fine map.

Connecting the lattice

Yesterday's lesson said that knowledge only becomes usable when hung on a latticework of models from many disciplines. This lesson explains why a lattice beats a single model: every model is a map that leaves something out, and the things one map omits are often exactly the things another map shows. The dashboard and the support inbox are two maps of the same system. The earnings statement and the cash flow statement are two maps of the same business. Holding several at once is how you find the blank spaces.

Tomorrow: the circle of competence, which is really the question of which parts of the map you have personally walked.

Keep

  • Every model, metric, report and theory is a map: useful because it leaves things out, dangerous when you forget what it left out.
  • Before trusting a map, ask three things: what it was drawn to show, what it omits that could matter here, and who drew it with what interest.
  • Keep a written list of the maps you rely on and the one thing each cannot see; that list is where your surprises will come from.
  • Over-applying the idea looks like refusing all models or demanding a one-to-one map. Use the cheap model where its errors are harmless and walk the ground where they are not.
  • Several maps of the same territory, laid over one another, reveal the blank spaces that any one of them hides. That is what a latticework is for.

Names worth knowing

  • Alfred Korzybski — Polish-American scholar whose 1933 book Science and Sanity founded general semantics; the source of the phrase and of the point that it applies to language and theory, not just paper.
  • George Box — British statistician who, in papers from 1976 onward, put the idea in the form that all models are wrong but some are useful.

Further reading

  • Science and Sanity, Alfred Korzybski, 1933 (the preface and first chapters are enough)
  • On Exactitude in Science, Jorge Luis Borges, 1946, a one-paragraph story
