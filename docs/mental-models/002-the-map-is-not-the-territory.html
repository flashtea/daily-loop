The map is not the territory

All models are wrong, some are useful, and the skill is knowing which part is wrong today

✓ read · 4 October 2026 · that day's edition · all mental models

Every description of reality leaves things out, and that is what makes it usable. Trouble starts when the user forgets which parts were left out, or who did the leaving. The lesson is about reading maps as maps: trusting them for what they were drawn to show, and checking the ground when the stakes are high.

A map is a model of a place, and it is usable because it leaves almost everything out: the smell, the traffic, the slope of the road, whether the bridge is open this morning. What remains is a small set of structured claims that fit in a pocket. The model works as long as the structure it keeps matches the structure of the territory in the respects that matter for the trip, and it fails when the user forgets that a reduction was ever made.

The phrase belongs to Alfred Korzybski, a Polish-American scholar, who wrote in 1931: "A map is not the territory it represents, but, if correct, it has a similar structure to the territory, which accounts for its usefulness." He was not writing about paper maps. He meant every word, number, chart and theory used to stand in for the world. The statistician George Box later compressed the same point into the remark that all models are wrong but some are useful, and both halves of that remark are needed.

Three maps that were read as the ground

A software dashboard shows every service green: processor load normal, error rate low, latency within bounds. Meanwhile users cannot log in, because a TLS certificate expired on a system the dashboard never measured. The dashboard was accurate. It had been drawn to show certain things, and the failure happened in the blank space. Teams that have been through this learn to ask what the dashboard cannot see, which is a different question from whether it is correct.

Financial statements are a map of a business drawn by accountants under rules written for other purposes. Enron's income statements were, for years, largely compliant with those rules and almost unrelated to the cash the business produced. Investors who read the earnings as the business were reading a map the mapmaker had drawn to be read that way. The ones who left early had gone to look at operating cash flow, at the debt parked in off-balance-sheet partnerships with names like JEDI and Chewco, and at what the company actually sold.

Three questions do most of the work. The first is what the map was drawn to show: a dashboard built for capacity planning will miss security, and an earnings figure built for tax comparability will miss cash. The second is what it left out and whether that could matter here; the answer is usually obvious once asked and invisible before. The third is who drew it and what they wanted the reader to see. Most maps are drawn by someone with an interest in the route. That does not make them wrong, and it does say where to be careful.

A practical habit is to keep a short written list of the maps relied on every day and, beside each, the one thing it cannot show. The error budget hides retries, the monthly report hides the bad week, and the benchmark hides whatever workload was left out of the benchmark. The list is short and rarely changes, so writing it once is enough.

Where the model misleads

It fails in two opposite directions. The first is the familiar one, treating the map as the ground. The second gets less attention and costs as much. A person who has learned that all maps are wrong refuses to use any, or demands a map so detailed that it stops being a map. Borges wrote a one-paragraph story, "On Exactitude in Science", about an empire whose cartographers produced a map at a scale of one to one; it was useless and was left to rot in the western deserts, where animals and beggars lived in its ruins. Someone who insists on seeing everything before deciding anything has built that map. The working skill is to use the cheap, wrong model for the ninety cases in which its errors do not matter and to go and look at the ground in the ten cases in which they do.

Over-application has a recognisable sound. The person says "that is just a model" to end an argument rather than to start one. The useful reply to "the map is wrong" is to ask wrong where, by how much, and whether it matters for this trip. A map that is wrong about the height of a hill nobody is climbing is a fine map.

Connection

Yesterday's lesson said knowledge becomes usable when hung on a lattice of models from many fields. This lesson supplies the reason: every model is a map that leaves something out, and what one map omits is often exactly what another shows. The dashboard and the support inbox are two maps of one system. The earnings statement and the cash-flow statement are two maps of one business. Holding several at once is how the blank spaces get found.
