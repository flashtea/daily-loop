Circle of competence

Knowing the edge of what you know, and what to do when you reach it

5 October 2026 · that day's edition · all mental models

Everyone has a region in which their judgment is reliable and a much larger region in which it only feels reliable. The model does not ask for a big circle; it asks for an honest line around it and different behaviour on each side. Most expensive mistakes are made a few steps outside the line by people who did not notice crossing it.

A circle of competence is the set of subjects in which a person's judgment is reliable: the base rates are known, the usual failure modes are known, and the questions that expose a bad argument come to mind without effort. Outside it, confidence and accuracy come apart, and the difference cannot usually be felt from the inside. The model asks for two things, an honest line and different behaviour on each side of it.

Warren Buffett set it out in his 1996 letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders. An investor does not need to understand many companies, only to evaluate the ones within their circle of competence, and the size of the circle matters far less than knowing where its perimeter runs. Munger's version is shorter: know the edge of your competency and stay inside it. The idea is much older than either of them. Socrates spent a career demonstrating to confident Athenians that they did not know what they believed they knew.

Inside the line

Three things are possible inside the circle and impossible outside it. A good explanation can be told from a merely plausible one. The frequency and direction of failure can be estimated. And the specific fact that would change one's mind can be named. An engineer reviewing a change in a codebase she has worked in for years does all three without noticing: this pattern leaks connections under load, that test will be flaky. The same engineer reading a term sheet for the first time has none of those reflexes, and the words look familiar, which is the problem.

Buffett's own record shows the model at work. Berkshire bought insurers, railroads, consumer brands and banks for fifty years and mostly avoided technology, on the stated ground that he could not tell which firms would still have a moat in ten years. The Apple position that began in 2016 was opened by one of his two investment managers, and Buffett later described Apple as a consumer products company with unusual customer loyalty. The circle had not grown to include semiconductors. The company had been described in terms that placed it inside the circle he already had.

Two domains, one failure

Medicine supplies the clearest cases. In 1998 a paper in The Lancet by Andrew Wakefield linked the MMR vaccine to autism on the basis of twelve children. Many well-educated parents accepted it. They could read the paper but could not evaluate it: twelve children is a case series, there was no control group, and the author had financial conflicts that came out years later, before the journal retracted the paper in 2010. Competence in the parents' own fields transferred confidence and nothing else.

Investing supplies the mirror image. During the boom in special-purpose acquisition companies in 2020 and 2021, many successful technology executives put money and their names behind SPACs in industries they had never worked in, among them electric trucks, space tourism and battery chemistry. Their records in software were real. Their ability to judge whether a start-up's claimed battery energy density was physically achievable did not exist, and most of those vehicles lost the greater part of their value within two years. Success in one circle feels, from the inside, like competence in general.

Finding the line

The boundary sits where calibration ends, usually some way inside where knowledge seems to end, and there are tests for it. One is whether the mechanism can be explained rather than the vocabulary; Richard Feynman's version was the difference between knowing the name of a thing and knowing the thing. Another is whether the base rate of failure can be stated, with three past instances. The most telling is whether the person has ever been wrong here and found out. A circle that has never been checked against outcomes is a hope with a boundary drawn around it.

At the edge

Most interesting decisions sit at or beyond the edge, and three moves are available there. The first is to refuse. Buffett keeps a "too hard" pile and treats putting something in it as a result. The second is to borrow a circle: find someone whose competence covers the ground and check that it does by the same tests, rather than by their confidence or their title. The third is to shrink the bet to a size at which being wrong is tuition rather than catastrophe. The default, carrying full inside-the-circle confidence across the line and acting on it at full size, is the move to avoid.

Where it misleads

Over-applied, the model becomes a reason never to learn anything. The circle is treated as fixed and every new subject is filed as too hard. Circles grow, through deliberate work at the boundary with small stakes and quick feedback, and Buffett and Munger spent decades enlarging theirs one industry at a time. Under-applied, which is far more common, the symptom is a person explaining a field they entered last month with the fluency reserved for their own. Someone more confident than people with twenty years in a domain should consider that the simplest explanation is not superior vision.

The habit

Before any decision that costs real money or time, write one sentence stating what is actually known about the matter and how that knowledge has been tested. If the honest answer is that the subject has been read about and makes sense, the decision is outside the circle, and one of the three moves should be chosen on purpose.

The model connects to the latticework and to the map and the territory. The latticework enlarges the circle by adding models that transfer between fields. The map and the territory warned that every model leaves things out; the circle of competence applies that warning to the reader's own understanding, which is also a map, with blank spaces of its own.
