Circle of competence

Knowing the edge of what you know, and what to do when you reach it

5 October 2026 · that day's edition · all mental models

Everyone has a region where their judgment is reliable and a much larger region where it only feels reliable. The model is not to make the circle big; it is to know where its boundary runs, and to behave differently on each side of it. Most expensive mistakes are made a few steps outside the line by people who did not notice crossing it.

A circle of competence is the set of things you understand well enough to predict how they will behave. Inside it, your judgment is worth something: you know the base rates, the failure modes, the questions that expose a bad argument. Outside it, your confidence and your accuracy come apart, and you usually cannot feel the difference from the inside. The model asks for two things. First, draw the boundary honestly. Second, act one way inside the line and another way outside it.

Warren Buffett put the idea in his 1996 shareholder letter: investors do not need to understand many companies, only to evaluate the ones within their circle of competence, and the size of the circle matters far less than knowing its perimeter. Charlie Munger said the same thing more bluntly: know the edge of your competency and stay inside it. The idea is older than either of them; Socrates built a career on showing confident Athenians that they did not know what they thought they knew.

What the circle looks like from inside

Inside the circle, you can do three things that you cannot do outside it. You can tell a good explanation from a plausible one. You can estimate how often things go wrong and in which direction. And you can name the specific fact that would change your mind. An engineer reviewing a pull request in a codebase they have worked in for years does all three without effort: this pattern leaks connections under load, this test will be flaky. The same engineer reading a term sheet for the first time has none of those reflexes. The words look familiar, which is exactly the problem.

Buffett's own record shows the model in use. Berkshire bought insurers, railroads, consumer brands and banks for fifty years and mostly skipped technology, not because technology was bad but because he could not predict which firms would still have their moat in a decade. When he finally bought Apple in 2016, he described it as a consumer products company with unusual customer loyalty: a description from inside his circle. The circle had not grown to include semiconductors; the company had been reframed to sit inside the circle he already had.

Two domains, one failure

Medicine offers the clearest cases of the boundary being crossed. In the early 2000s a widely publicized study linking vaccines to autism was accepted by many well-educated parents who could read the paper but not evaluate it: they could not see that twelve children is not a sample, that the controls were missing, or that the author had undisclosed conflicts. Their competence in their own fields transferred confidence but not skill.

Investing supplies the mirror image. During the 2021 boom in special-purpose acquisition companies, many successful technology executives put money and their names behind SPACs in industries they had never operated in: electric trucks, space tourism, battery chemistry. Their record in software was real. Their ability to judge whether a startup's claimed battery energy density was achievable was not, and most of those vehicles lost the majority of their value within two years. They did not fail because they were foolish. They failed because success in one circle feels, from the inside, like competence in general.

Finding the boundary

The boundary is not where your knowledge ends but where your calibration ends, and there are tests for it. Can you explain the mechanism, not just the vocabulary? Richard Feynman's version was to distinguish knowing the name of something from knowing the thing. Can you say what the base rate of failure is and name three ways this has failed before? Have you ever been wrong here and found out? That last one matters most: a circle that has never been tested against outcomes is not a circle, it is a hope.

What to do at the edge

The model is only half useful if it just tells you to stay home. Most interesting decisions are at or beyond the edge, and three moves are available there. The first is to refuse: Buffett calls this the too-hard pile and treats it as a legitimate outcome. The second is to borrow a circle: find someone whose competence covers the ground, and check that it does by the same tests you applied to yourself, not by their confidence or title. The third is to shrink the bet to a size at which being wrong is an education rather than a catastrophe, and call it tuition.

What you should not do is the default, which is to carry your inside-the-circle confidence across the line and act on it at full size.

The failure mode

The model misleads in two directions. Over-applied, it becomes an excuse never to learn anything new: the circle is treated as fixed and every unfamiliar problem is declared too hard. Circles grow, and they grow by deliberate work at the boundary with small stakes and fast feedback; Buffett and Munger spent decades expanding theirs one industry at a time. Under-applied, which is far more common, the symptom is a person explaining a field they entered last month with the fluency they use for their own. If you are more confident than people who have worked in a domain for twenty years, the likeliest explanation is not that you see further.

The habit

Before any decision that costs real money or real time, write one sentence: what do I actually know about this, and how do I know that I know it? If the answer is that you have read about it and it makes sense, you are outside the circle. Then choose one of the three moves on purpose.

This connects to the two lessons before it. The latticework grows the circle by adding models that transfer across fields. The map is not the territory warns that every model leaves things out; the circle of competence applies that warning to yourself, because your own understanding is a map too, and the part it leaves out is where the money goes.

Keep

  • The size of your circle matters less than knowing where its edge is; most costly errors happen just outside it, unnoticed.
  • Test the boundary with mechanism, base rates and past outcomes, not with fluency; knowing the names of things is not knowing them.
  • Success inside one circle feels like general competence from the inside; the feeling is the warning, not the evidence.
  • At the edge you have three honest moves: refuse, borrow a verified circle, or shrink the bet to tuition size.
  • Circles grow, but by deliberate low-stakes work at the boundary, not by walking across it at full confidence.

Names worth knowing

  • Warren Buffett — Set out the idea in Berkshire Hathaway's 1996 letter: what counts is knowing the perimeter of your circle, not its size.
  • Charlie Munger — Buffett's partner, who paired the circle with the latticework: know your edge and stay inside it.
  • Richard Feynman — Physicist whose distinction between knowing the name of a thing and knowing the thing is the best test for the boundary.
