First-principles reasoning

Reason from what you know to be true, not from what everyone else does, and know when borrowing is the smarter move

8 October 2026 · that day's edition · all mental models

First-principles reasoning means taking a problem apart until it rests on facts known to be true on their own, then building the answer up from those, instead of copying what similar things cost or how similar things are done. It is the tool that finds ten-fold improvements, and it is expensive, so most of the skill lies in knowing when analogy is the better bet.

Most beliefs about how things should be done are held by analogy. A rocket costs what rockets have cost. A feature ships in a quarter because features ship in quarters. Reasoning by analogy is fast and usually right, which is why people do it all day. First-principles reasoning is the deliberate refusal to do it for a while. The question is taken apart until it reaches things that are true on their own, such as physical constants, the prices of raw inputs, what a customer actually said or what a law actually states, and the answer is rebuilt from those parts only.

The name is old. Aristotle called them archai, the first things: the premises from which a subject is demonstrated and which cannot be derived from anything more basic. In physics they are conservation laws and material properties; in a business, input costs and what people will pay; in a software system, latency, bandwidth, memory and the work the computation actually requires. The phrase's modern currency owes much to Elon Musk, who has described the method in interviews as boiling a problem down to its fundamental truths and reasoning up from there, and who credits it for the cost estimates behind SpaceX and Tesla.

Two cases

The rocket is the famous one. In the early 2000s a launch to orbit cost tens of millions of dollars, and the analogy said that this was what rockets cost. Musk's team instead priced the raw materials, aerospace-grade aluminium, titanium, copper and carbon fibre, and found that they came to a small fraction of the finished price, by his account around two percent. The gap was not in the physics. It was in how rockets were built, bought and thrown away after one use, and that single piece of arithmetic is the whole argument for building them differently and landing them. The same move produced his battery estimate. Instead of accepting that packs cost 600 dollars a kilowatt-hour, the team asked what lithium, cobalt, nickel and aluminium cost on the metals exchange per kilowatt-hour of cell, and got a number several times lower, which became the target.

Software gives a quieter example. A team decides that its nightly reporting job is slow and that the fix is a cluster, because clusters are what large data jobs run on. A first-principles pass asks how much data there is (a few gigabytes), how fast one machine reads it from disk (gigabytes per second) and how much arithmetic the report needs (very little). The job should take seconds on a laptop, and the slowness is in the code. Engineers who estimate from bytes and nanoseconds find the order-of-magnitude wins. The others buy the cluster and get a slightly faster slow job.

How to do it

The first step is to write down the assumption about to be inherited: this costs X, this needs a database. The second is to ask what the assumption is made of and to keep asking until the answer is something that can be verified directly, such as a price list, a measurement, a law of nature or a statement from the person who will pay. The third is to rebuild from those pieces and compare the result with the inherited number. A small gap means the analogy was fine and an hour was spent confirming it. A large gap is the finding, and the next question is what, specifically, consumes the difference.

People stop early at the second step. "The vendor charges this" is someone else's analogy, and so is "the benchmark says". Bedrock is reached when the premise cannot be reduced further without leaving the reasoner's own competence.

Where it misleads

First-principles reasoning fails in two directions. The first is cost. Deriving everything from scratch is slow, and most of the time analogy would have given the same answer. A company that insists on first principles for every decision never ships. Analogy is how a civilisation stores answers so that each person is spared recomputing them, and refusing the store entirely is waste.

The second failure is more dangerous: reasoning confidently from the wrong fundamentals. The rocket arithmetic worked because material cost really is the floor for a mass-produced object. The same arithmetic applied to drug development, where the floor is set by biology and by how many candidate molecules fail in trials, produces a fantasy. Over-application shows as an elegant chain of logic from untrue premises, contempt for precedent, and repeated surprise at failure modes any practitioner could have named. Chesterton's fence is the corrective: the thing everybody does is often there for a reason the reasoner has not yet found.

Analogy is the better tool when the reference class is large and the thing being done is ordinary. For a kitchen renovation, the hundred renovations that overran are better evidence than any bottom-up plan, because the plan omits exactly what made them overrun. First principles belong where the gap between inherited cost and physical floor is suspiciously large, or where the thing intended has never been done. Analogy belongs where the activity is routine and the record is long.

The habit

Pick one number about to be accepted this week, a quote, an estimate or a budget line, and spend ten minutes asking what it is made of. Write down the components and the cost of each from a trusted source, then compare the total with the quote. Usually they roughly match, and the habit pays for itself on the one occasion in ten when they differ by a factor of five.

Connections

The map-and-territory lesson applies here to inherited answers: an analogy is a map someone else drew, and first principles means walking the ground. The method depends on the circle of competence, because a premise is a first principle only if the reasoner can verify it. It pairs with inversion: having built the number up, ask what would have to be false for it to be wrong. And it opens a tension that runs through the whole lattice, between bottom-up reasoning that finds the exceptional case and base-rate reasoning that protects in the ordinary one. Probabilistic thinking, the outside view and Chesterton's fence are all ways of managing that tension.
