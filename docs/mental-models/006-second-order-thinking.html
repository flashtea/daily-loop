Second-order thinking

And then what? Following consequences past the first step, and knowing when to stop

12 October 2026 · that day's edition · all mental models

Second-order thinking is the habit of asking what the effects of a decision will cause in turn, after the people and systems affected have responded. First-order thinking stops at the obvious result. Second-order thinking follows the chain one or two steps further, and it is the difference between a policy that works on paper and one that works on people.

Every action has an immediate effect, and that effect has effects of its own. First-order thinking considers only the first link: cut the price and sell more, add a bonus and get more effort, ban the thing and have less of it. Second-order thinking asks what happens next, once customers, competitors, employees, markets and the system itself have had time to respond to the first effect. Howard Marks, in his memos to Oaktree clients, gave the question its plainest form: and then what.

The idea was a complaint before it was a method. Frédéric Bastiat, a French economist writing in 1850, divided economists into the bad ones who see only the visible effect and the good ones who also account for the effects that must be foreseen. His example was a shopkeeper's broken window. The glazier gains, which everyone sees; the shopkeeper would otherwise have bought shoes, which nobody sees. The glazier's trade is the first-order effect, and the shoes never bought are the second.

Two cases

Colonial India gives the textbook case, usually told as the cobra effect. The British administration in Delhi, troubled by venomous snakes, is said to have paid a bounty per dead cobra. The first-order effect was dead cobras. The second-order effect was a breeding industry, and when the government noticed and cancelled the bounty, the breeders released their worthless stock, leaving more cobras than before. The Delhi story is poorly documented. A well-recorded version happened in Hanoi in 1902, where the French colonial government paid a bounty per rat tail and the city's sewers filled with tailless rats, released alive to breed more tails. Whoever pays for a proxy should ask what someone will do to manufacture the proxy.

Software has its version in retries. A service is overloaded, so the team adds a rule: if a request fails, the client retries at once. The first-order effect is that transient errors heal themselves. The second-order effect, which operators meet during an outage, is that a struggling server now receives several times its normal traffic as every client hammers it in the same second, and a brief wobble becomes a collapse. The standard fix, exponential backoff with random jitter, exists because somebody followed the chain one step further and asked what a million clients do when they all get the same error at the same moment.

Interest rates show the same shape. A central bank raises rates to slow inflation; that is the first order. At the second order, floating-rate borrowers have less to spend, governments pay more on their debt, banks holding long bonds sit on paper losses, currencies move, and emerging markets that borrowed in dollars face a squeeze. The failure of Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023 was in part a second-order effect of the 2022 rate rises on a balance sheet full of long-dated bonds bought when rates were near zero. The people who saw it coming were not forecasting inflation. They were asking what a fast rise in rates would do to whoever was holding what.

How to do it

The mechanics are simple and the discipline is the hard part. Write down the immediate effect being aimed for. List who or what reacts to that effect: customers, competitors, employees, regulators, the system's own feedback loops. For each of them, ask what they would do in response if acting in their own interest, and write that down. Then ask the question once more about the most important of those responses. Stop where the answers turn from reasoning into guessing, which is usually after two steps.

A useful variant runs over time rather than across actors. Marks's first-level thinker says a company is good, so buy the stock. His second-level thinker says the company is good, but everyone already thinks so, so the price reflects it and the stock is no bargain. In markets the second-order effect of good news is that it is already in the price. The same holds for a career move, a hire or an architectural choice: the obvious benefit is the one everyone else also saw and acted on.

Where it misleads

The failure mode is paralysis dressed as rigour. Every chain of consequences can be extended indefinitely, and each added step is less certain than the one before. Someone over-applying the model never ships the price cut because a fifth-order effect might conceivably hurt, or blocks a hire over a hypothetical reorganisation two years out. The tell is a long argument against a simple action whose later steps rest on nothing that could be checked. At that point reasoning has turned into storytelling, and the right move is to run a cheap, reversible version of the action and watch what the system actually does.

A second trap is assuming the second-order effect always cancels the first. It often does not. Vaccination programmes, compound interest and well-designed incentives all have second-order effects that reinforce the first. The tool is for seeing the whole chain, and it gives no special support to the conclusion that nothing should be done.

Connections

Second-order thinking is inversion with the clock running forward. Inversion asks what would make a plan fail; second-order thinking asks what the plan will cause, and the two answers usually overlap. It leans on the map and the territory, because the mental model of how a system responds is the map, and the second-order effect is where the territory tends to differ from it. It depends on the circle of competence for knowing when to stop following the chain; beyond the edge of what the reasoner knows, the next link is a guess. And it is the gateway to the incentives tendency and the systems models later in the series, which are second-order thinking made specific.

A habit for tomorrow: before any decision that affects other people, state the intended effect, ask what happens next twice, and write down both answers. If the second answer is bad and the first was only convenient, the decision is made.
