Occam's razor

Simpler explanations and the cost of extra assumptions

22 October 2026 · that day's edition · all mental models

Occam's razor says that when two explanations fit the same evidence, the one that needs fewer independent assumptions is more likely to be right, because each assumption is one more thing that could be false. It is a rule for ranking hypotheses, and it works best when the simpler story also makes a sharper prediction. It fails on patients, markets and systems that are allowed to have several things wrong at once.

Occam's razor is a rule for choosing between explanations that fit the same facts: prefer the one that needs the fewest separate assumptions. The reasoning behind it is arithmetic. Each assumption an explanation requires is a claim that could be false, and a story that needs four such claims to hold at once has more ways to be wrong than a story that needs one. The rule says nothing about which explanation is true; it says which one to bet on first, and which one must carry the burden of extra evidence.

The name comes from William of Ockham, a Franciscan friar born in Surrey around 1287 who taught at Oxford and died in Munich in 1347. The Latin tag usually attached to him, that entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity, appears in no surviving text of his; the Irish Franciscan John Punch wrote it in 1639 as a summary of Ockham's method. Ockham's own wording was that plurality should never be posited without necessity.

Counting assumptions, not words

The razor measures assumptions, and an assumption is a claim about the world that the explanation requires and the evidence does not supply. A short sentence can hide a dozen of them, and a long one can rest on a single one, so the length of the explanation is no guide. The useful habit is to write each competing story down and list, under each, every independent fact that would have to be true for the story to hold.

Ptolemy's model of the planets, written in Alexandria around 150 AD, put the earth at the centre and explained each planet's backward loops with a small circle, the epicycle, riding on a larger one. The detail that does not fit the usual telling is that Copernicus, in 1543, still needed epicycles, and about as many of them, because he kept circular orbits. The real shaving came with Johannes Kepler in 1609, who replaced every circle-on-circle for Mars with one ellipse after Tycho Brahe's observations of Mars disagreed with the best circular fit by eight minutes of arc. Eight minutes was within what Ptolemy would have accepted and outside what Kepler would, and the razor cut only once the evidence was fine enough to make the extra assumptions visible.

A line at forty-five degrees

In 1999 Harry Markopolos, a portfolio manager at Rampart Investment Management in Boston, was handed the return stream of a fund run by Bernard Madoff and asked to build a product that matched it. Madoff's stated method was a split-strike conversion: buy a basket of large stocks, sell call options above the price, buy put options below. The returns ran at about one percent a month with only a handful of down months in more than a decade, a line rising at close to forty-five degrees on the chart, and the options volume needed to hedge a fund of that size exceeded what traded on the whole Chicago Board Options Exchange.

Two explanations fit the chart, and they differ in what they require. One was that Madoff had found an edge in options markets that no one else could see, kept it secret for fifteen years, executed it at a scale the exchanges could not accommodate, and did so while leaving the fees to feeder funds. The other was that the statements were typed. Markopolos sent his conclusion to the Securities and Exchange Commission in May 2000, again in 2001, and in a 2005 memo titled "The World's Largest Hedge Fund is a Fraud". The SEC found nothing until Madoff confessed to his sons in December 2008, with about 64.8 billion dollars of fabricated balances on client statements.

When the razor cuts the wrong thing

The razor fails where the world is allowed to have several things wrong at once. John Hickam, a professor of medicine at Duke in the 1950s, is credited with the reply that doctors still teach beside the razor: a patient can have as many diseases as they damn well please. In a seventy-year-old with fatigue, anaemia and a cough, the single explanation may be a tumour, and it may also be three ordinary conditions arriving together, and the base rate of three common things exceeds the base rate of one rare thing more often than intuition allows. The razor works on independent assumptions; it misleads when the "assumptions" are common events that stack.

Systems mislead it in the same way. An explanation of the 2008 crisis that names one cause is simpler than one that names subprime underwriting, rating-agency incentives, the repo market and leverage at the broker-dealers, and it is worse, because a failure of that kind has several necessary conditions and the removal of any one would have changed the outcome. The over-applier can be recognised by the phrase "it all comes down to". Simplicity is also relative to the vocabulary in use: a story that looks simple in one set of terms can need a dozen hidden clauses in another.

The question to carry

When two stories fit the evidence, list what each requires to be true, and then ask what each one predicts that the other does not. The second half of the question is the sharper half. Markopolos did both: the fraud story required few assumptions, and it also predicted that the options volume would never be found, which was checkable. A simple explanation that predicts nothing new is a preference; a simple explanation that predicts something testable is a hypothesis.

The razor sits close to several models already in the series. Bayesian updating contains it in disguise, since a hypothesis with more adjustable parts spreads its probability over more possible outcomes and gains less from any one of them, so a Bayesian prefers simpler models without being told to. Probabilistic thinking supplies the base rates that decide when Hickam's dictum applies instead, and the second lesson's caution against trusting a map for its tidiness applies here as well.
