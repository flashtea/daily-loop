The latticework

Why isolated facts don't stick, and what to build instead

✓ read · 3 October 2026 · that day's edition · all mental models

Charlie Munger's central idea: knowledge becomes usable only when the big models from many disciplines are hung on one frame and checked against each other. Someone holding a single model forces every problem into it, and someone holding eighty can see which model fits the problem in front of them.

In 1994 Charlie Munger gave a talk at the University of Southern California's business school called "A Lesson on Elementary, Worldly Wisdom." The claim at its centre was that most educated people carry a great many facts and very little usable knowledge, because the facts are stored as a list rather than attached to a structure. His sentence for it was: "If the facts don't hang together on a latticework of theory, you don't have them in a usable form."

A lattice, in his sense, is a frame in which each piece is fixed to several others, so that a load on one point is carried by the whole and a piece that does not fit shows up as a piece that does not fit. He proposed that an educated person needs roughly eighty to ninety models, taken from the disciplines that have produced reliable results, among them mathematics, physics, biology, psychology, engineering and economics, and that inside each discipline only a handful of ideas carry most of the weight. Compounding comes from mathematics, margin of safety from engineering, incentives from psychology and economics. The rest of a discipline can be looked up when needed.

One tool for every problem

Munger's name for the opposite condition was the man-with-a-hammer tendency, after the old saying that to a man with only a hammer every problem looks like a nail. The economist explains a divorce with incentives, the psychologist explains a market crash with fear, and the programmer explains every organisational failure as a missing abstraction. Each of them is sometimes right, and none of them can tell when, because a single model offers no second reading to compare against. The condition is worse in experts than in beginners. An expert's one model has worked often enough to earn trust, and the trust extends past the range in which it was earned. Munger's remedy was to own more tools, so that reaching for one makes the unused ones visible and raises the question of why they stayed in the box.

Why the models have to come from several fields

Each discipline teaches the models that work inside it and leaves out the ones that belong to the department next door. Munger said his own business education had contained almost no psychology, so he assembled his own list of the standard causes of human misjudgment, twenty-five of them by his final count, from Robert Cialdini's work on influence and from forty years of watching people lose money. Economics departments assumed rational agents for most of the twentieth century because the evidence against that assumption sat in psychology. Engineers learn margin of safety in their first year; the risk models at the banks that failed in 2008 did not contain the idea.

The models that pay best are the ones that apply across fields. Compounding describes a bank balance, a forest and a skill. A feedback loop describes a thermostat, a bank run and an addiction. Natural selection explains why species change and also why a badly run company can survive for years in a market with few competitors and then disappear within months when a strong one arrives. When a model from physics and a model from psychology predict the same outcome independently, the prediction deserves more weight than either would earn alone. Munger called the large version of this a lollapalooza: several forces pushing the same way at once, which is where the biggest effects, good and bad, come from.

How a lattice gets built

The first habit is to learn each model with examples from at least two unrelated fields, so that it is stored as a pattern and not as a fact about its home subject. A person who knows compounding only from finance will not recognise it in a codebase whose technical debt grows by a steady percentage a year. The second habit is to run a new problem past several models on purpose and to note which ones have something to say. The silent ones are information too; a problem about which compounding, incentives and feedback all have nothing to say is probably not an economic problem. The third habit is to watch for the lollapalooza, because a situation in which four models agree is also the situation in which the person holding one model is most badly surprised.

This series is arranged on that plan. The first twenty lessons are general tools for thinking. The psychological tendencies follow, one a day, because misjudgment produces most of the expensive mistakes. Then come mathematics and the physical sciences, which supply the most general models, then economics, systems and engineering, and at the end decision-making under uncertainty, with cases in which many models act together.

Munger's test

Munger had a test for whether a model is held rather than merely recognised. The holder should be able to state the strongest case against their own position better than the people who hold that position can. His version, from the same talk, was: "I never allow myself to have an opinion on anything that I don't know the other side's argument better than they do." By that standard a model one cannot argue against is a slogan, and a model one can argue against and still find useful is a working part of the lattice.

Tomorrow's lesson takes up the first of the general tools, the map and the territory, which supplies the reason a lattice of many models beats one good one.
