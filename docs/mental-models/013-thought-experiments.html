Thought experiments

Galileo's stones, Einstein's elevator, and cheap experiments run in the head

26 October 2026 · that day's edition · all mental models

A thought experiment runs an imagined apparatus on the rules you already accept and sees whether they hold together: Galileo's two stones showed one rule giving two answers, Einstein's sealed chest showed two situations giving one. The same move exposes sunk cost in a boardroom and hidden risks in a plan. It tests only the consistency of your assumptions, so it has to be followed by a real test.

A thought experiment is an experiment run on an imagined apparatus, with the laws you already accept doing the work of the equipment. You set up a situation precisely, usually one that cannot be built or would cost too much to build, and follow what your own assumptions say must happen inside it. The result is either a prediction you can go and test, or a contradiction showing that two of your assumptions cannot both be true. The Dutch engineer Simon Stevin used it in 1586 to derive the law of the inclined plane; Galileo and Einstein overturned physics with it without, at the decisive moments, touching an instrument.

Galileo's two stones

In the Discorsi of 1638 Galileo attacks the Aristotelian claim that heavier bodies fall faster. Take a heavy stone and a light one; on Aristotle's view the heavy one falls faster. Now tie them together with a short cord. The light stone, falling more slowly, should drag on the heavy one and slow it, so the pair falls more slowly than the heavy stone alone. But the pair is heavier than the heavy stone, so on the same view it should fall faster. One rule has produced two incompatible predictions, so the rule is wrong, and the only rate that escapes the contradiction is one that does not depend on weight.

Stevin's version half a century earlier imagined a closed loop of fourteen identical balls on a string draped over a triangular prism: four balls on the long gentle slope, two on the short steep one, eight hanging in a loop underneath. If the slopes exerted unequal pulls the loop would turn by itself forever, a perpetual motion he regarded as absurd, so the pulls must balance, and the force along a slope must fall in proportion to the slope's length. He put the diagram on his title page with the motto that wonder is no wonder.

Einstein's elevator

In 1907, writing a review for the Jahrbuch der Radioaktivität, Einstein imagined a physicist sealed in a chest with no windows. If the chest rests on Earth, objects released inside it fall to the floor at 9.8 metres per second squared. If the chest is far from any planet and a rope is pulling it upward with that same acceleration, released objects also fall to the floor at 9.8 metres per second squared, and no experiment inside can tell the two cases apart. Einstein took the indistinguishability as a law, the equivalence principle, and from it followed that gravity must bend light and slow clocks. Eddington measured the bending from Príncipe in 1919; every GPS receiver corrects for the clock effect, about 38 microseconds a day.

The same tool in a boardroom

In mid-1985 Intel was losing money in memory chips, the business it had been founded on in 1968, to Japanese producers selling below cost. Andy Grove later wrote in Only the Paranoid Survive that he asked Gordon Moore what a new chief executive would do if the board threw the two of them out and brought in an outsider. Moore answered, in substance, that the new man would take Intel out of memories, and Grove replied, again in substance, that they could walk out the door, come back in and do it themselves. The exit from DRAM followed. The imagined apparatus was an outsider with no history and no sunk cost, and running it showed that the only thing holding Intel in memories was the identity of the people deciding.

Gary Klein's pre-mortem, published in the Harvard Business Review in 2007, is the procedural version. Before a plan launches, the team is told that a year has passed and the plan has failed completely, and each person writes down alone, in two minutes, the reasons. In the imagined world the failure is a fact to be explained rather than an insult to the plan's author, so risks surface that a forward-looking review suppresses. Klein's evidence is a 1989 study by Mitchell, Russo and Pennington in which explaining an outcome as if it had already happened raised the number of reasons named by about 30 percent.

Where it misleads

A thought experiment tests only the consistency of the assumptions fed into it. Aristotle's physics was a system of thought experiments too, and it stood for two thousand years because the assumptions were wrong and nobody ran the real test. Galileo's two stones work because the hidden assumption, that the cord adds nothing and the stones ignore the air, is nearly true for stones. For feathers the argument fails in practice, and the feather settles the matter.

The second failure is the smuggled intuition. Daniel Dennett called the genre intuition pumps, and many of the famous philosophical ones, Searle's Chinese room of 1980 among them, remain contested because the scenario is built so that one answer feels obvious before any reasoning begins. When a thought experiment delivers a conclusion that no measurement can follow up, suspicion is the right response, and the over-applier is the person arguing from a scenario that was chosen after the conclusion. The third failure is stopping at the imagined version because it costs nothing, when the two stones were in the end dropped from real towers and Eddington spent months reaching Príncipe.

A habit

Before the next irreversible decision, run Grove's version in writing: describe the situation as a newly arrived outsider would see it, with no knowledge of how it came about, and write the first paragraph of the memo that person would send. Then run Klein's: the decision was taken, a year has passed, it failed, and the first paragraph of the post-mortem reads as follows. The two paragraphs usually disagree with the plan in at least one place that can be checked for real.

Thought experiments sit beside inversion, which asks what would guarantee failure, and first-principles reasoning, which supplies the rules the imagined apparatus runs on; the map-and-territory lesson explains why the result still has to be checked against the ground.
