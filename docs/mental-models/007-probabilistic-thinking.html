Probabilistic thinking

Thinking in bets, base rates, and the difference between a good decision and a good outcome

14 October 2026 · that day's edition · all mental models

Probabilistic thinking is the habit of holding beliefs as degrees of confidence and of choosing by weighing what could happen against how likely each outcome is. It separates the quality of a decision from the quality of its result, because the two come apart whenever chance is involved. The tool is a number, even a rough one, attached to every claim that matters.

Probabilistic thinking is the habit of holding beliefs as degrees of confidence rather than as certainties, and of deciding by weighing what could happen against how likely each outcome is. A belief held this way carries a number, even a rough one: seventy percent, one in five, nearly certain. The habit has two roots. One is the mathematics of chance, which Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat worked out in letters in 1654 over how to split the stakes of a dice game stopped before it ended. The other is the practice of bookmakers and marine insurers, who learned that a person who says seventy percent can at least be checked.

In use the model has three parts: attach a probability to the claim instead of a yes or a no; start from the base rate, meaning how often things of this kind have turned out this way before, rather than from the vivid details of the case in hand; and judge a decision by the information and odds available when it was made, separately from its outcome, because a good bet loses sometimes and a bad bet sometimes pays.

The bettor's stance

Annie Duke won a World Series of Poker bracelet in 2004 and spent two decades at tables where every hand was a decision under uncertainty with money attached. In Thinking in Bets, published in 2018, she named the error that poker trains out of people and ordinary life trains into them: resulting, the grading of a decision by how it turned out. Her example was a football play. In the Super Bowl of 1 February 2015, the Seattle Seahawks had the ball on the New England one-yard line with 26 seconds left, trailing by four. Coach Pete Carroll called a pass; Malcolm Butler intercepted it and New England won. The call was named the worst in Super Bowl history the next morning. Duke's point was that the pass had a decent case, since it stopped the clock and kept a running play available for the next down, and passes from the one-yard line that season had, by one count, been intercepted close to never. The decision was defensible and the outcome was terrible.

The same split shows up in a software team's incident reviews. A deploy goes out on a Friday afternoon and the site is down for an hour. The question worth asking afterwards is whether the deploy was a reasonable bet given the test coverage and the rollback path. Sometimes it was a sound bet that lost, and the right change is nothing. Sometimes it was reckless and had happened to work the previous forty times, and the right change is large. A team that reviews only the deploys that failed will fix its luck and leave its judgment alone.

Base rates

The second part, starting from the base rate, has the longest evidence behind it. In 1954 the psychologist Paul Meehl published a review of about twenty studies comparing clinicians' predictions with simple statistical formulas on the same cases, and the formulas matched or beat the clinicians in nearly every one. The clinicians had more information and used it worse.

Calibration is the test of whether the numbers mean anything. Of all the events a person calls seventy percent likely, about seventy percent should happen. Allan Murphy and Robert Winkler checked this for United States weather forecasters in 1977 and found them close to perfectly calibrated, with a plain explanation: forecasters make a numerical forecast every day, see the answer the next day, and keep score. Philip Tetlock's forecasting tournaments between 2011 and 2015 found that amateurs who kept score the same way were reportedly about thirty percent more accurate than intelligence analysts with access to classified material.

Munger at the racetrack

Charlie Munger's favourite picture of a market was the pari-mutuel system at a racetrack. The odds on each horse are set by the money bet on it, so the obvious favourite pays little and the obvious loser pays a lot, and the track takes about seventeen percent off the top. Winning requires a horse whose real chance is higher than the crowd's odds imply. Berkshire Hathaway's super-catastrophe reinsurance in the 1990s was the same stance applied to hurricanes: a premium collected every year against an enormous payout in the rare year a storm hits Florida. Warren Buffett wrote in his letters of that period that some years would show large losses and that this proved nothing about whether the price had been right.

Where it misleads

The model fails first through false precision. A number is only as good as the reference class behind it, and some events have no reference class worth the name: a technology that has never shipped, a war between two countries that have never fought. Writing down forty percent for such a thing records a mood, and Frank Knight drew this line in 1921 between risk, which can be priced, and uncertainty, which cannot. The second failure is probability as alibi: someone who skipped the research and lost can say it was a seventy percent bet, so the defence is to ask what work produced the number. The third failure is ruin. A ninety percent bet made once with everything is a different animal from the same bet made a hundred times with a little, and the gambler who bets the house at good odds is right on average and broke in fact.

A habit for tomorrow

Before the next decision that matters, write down a probability for the outcome and a sentence on its source. After the outcome is known, grade the decision by the note and the outcome by itself. Over a few months the notes become a calibration record, and they show whether the seventy percents are coming true seventy percent of the time.

Second-order thinking, from the previous lesson, supplies the branches of what might happen; this lesson supplies the weights on each branch. The next lesson, on Bayesian updating, is about how to move the weight when new evidence arrives, and by how much.
