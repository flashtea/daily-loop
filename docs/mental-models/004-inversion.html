Inversion

Invert, always invert: solve problems backward, and avoid stupidity before you seek brilliance

6 October 2026 · that day's edition · all mental models

Many problems that are hard to solve forward are easy to solve backward. Instead of asking how to reach a goal, ask what would guarantee failure, and then arrange for none of those things to happen. The model comes from mathematics, was made a rule of life by Charlie Munger, and works because minds generate failure modes far more reliably than they generate winning strategies.

Inversion is the habit of turning a question around before trying to answer it. Instead of asking how a project can be made to succeed, ask what would make it fail for certain, list the answers, and make sure none of them happen. Instead of asking how to get rich, ask how people with this income end up broke. The forward question invites wishful thinking and a search through an unlimited space of clever moves. The inverted question produces a short, concrete list within minutes, and most of the items on it are under the asker's control.

The slogan comes from the nineteenth-century mathematician Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi, who is credited with the maxim "man muss immer umkehren", one must always invert. Jacobi meant it technically: many results in analysis fall out once the problem is restated in inverse form. Charlie Munger borrowed the phrase and made it a rule of life. His version, repeated in many talks, was that all he wanted to know was where he was going to die, so that he could avoid going there. The joke carries the method. A map of the cliffs is enough for a long time, and it is far easier to draw than a map of every road to the summit.

Why backward is easier than forward

There is an asymmetry in what minds are good at. Asked for a strategy to win, most people produce vague optimism. Asked how something could go wrong, the same people produce a precise list in seconds: the supplier who is a single point of failure, the assumption nobody tested, the colleague who was never told. Evolution tuned people to spot threats rather than to design triumphs, and inversion points that strength at the problem.

There is a second asymmetry in the problems themselves. In most endeavours there are many ways to fail and few ways to win, and the failures repeat across people and eras while the wins are specific and often lucky. Removing a known cause of failure is therefore more dependable than adding a hoped-for cause of success. Munger called this avoiding stupidity rather than seeking brilliance, and he was clear about which of the two had made him his money.

Two domains, the same move

Engineering has institutionalised inversion under other names. A failure mode and effects analysis, a procedure the United States military wrote into a standard in 1949, asks in what ways a part could fail, how likely each way is and how bad the result would be, and scores the three to decide where to spend attention. A pre-mortem, which the psychologist Gary Klein described in 2007, asks a project team to imagine that a year has passed and the project has failed badly, then to write the history of that failure. Teams raise objections in a pre-mortem that they would never raise in a planning meeting, because the exercise makes it safe to be the pessimist.

Investing offers the same lesson with a longer record. The question of which stock will go up has no reliable answer. The question of what reliably destroys a portfolio does: leverage that forces a sale at the bottom, concentration in a story the holder cannot verify, fees that compound against the holder, and trading on news everyone else already has. An investor who merely refuses all four has removed most of the ways to lose without having had a single good idea. Warren Buffett's two rules, never lose money and never forget rule one, are inversion dressed as a joke.

Software has its own version. Asking how to make a service reliable can absorb a month of architecture discussion. Asking what would page the on-call engineer at three in the morning produces the unbounded queue, the retry storm, the configuration that differs between staging and production, and the dependency with no timeout, and those get fixed first.

Where inversion misleads

Inversion misleads when avoiding the bad becomes the whole plan. A company that only asks what could go wrong never ships, because everything could. A person organised around never being embarrassed avoids every situation in which they might have grown. Removing causes of failure is necessary and underrated, and it is still insufficient: at some point the forward thing has to be done, and no list of cliffs says where the summit is.

Over-application has a texture. The inverted list has turned into a veto, every item carries the same weight regardless of likelihood, and the meeting ends with nothing decided. The fix is to pair inversion with probability: of the ten ways this could fail, which three carry most of the risk, and what does each cost to remove. A pre-mortem that produces thirty objections and no ranking has not finished. Some forward questions should also stay forward. How to delight a customer is a different question from how to avoid annoying one, and a product built only from the second is merely inoffensive.

A habit for tomorrow

Take one goal for this week and write down, in five minutes, the three most dependable ways to make sure it does not happen. Be specific: "the client learns about the delay from the invoice" rather than "poor communication". Then check whether the current plan already does any of them. It usually does one.

The deeper habit is to notice being stuck on a forward question and to try its inverse, which is a question about how the world already fails.

Connections

Inversion is the natural partner of the circle of competence from yesterday. In an unfamiliar domain it is rarely possible to say what will work and almost always possible to say what has failed there before, so inversion is the first tool to reach for at the edge of the circle. Later in the series the pre-mortem returns as the defence against the planning fallacy, and Munger's list of psychological tendencies is itself an inverted project: a catalogue of the standard ways a mind goes wrong, compiled so that a reader can check their own against it.
