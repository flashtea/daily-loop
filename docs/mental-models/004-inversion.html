Inversion

Invert, always invert: solve problems backward, and avoid stupidity before you seek brilliance

6 October 2026 · that day's edition · all mental models

Many problems that are hard to solve forward are easy to solve backward. Instead of asking how to reach a goal, ask what would guarantee failure, and then arrange not to do those things. The model comes from mathematics, was popularized by Charlie Munger, and works because our minds generate failure modes far more reliably than they generate winning strategies.

Inversion is the habit of turning a question around before trying to answer it. Instead of asking "how do I make this project succeed?", ask "what would make it fail for certain?", list the answers, and make sure none of them happen. Instead of "how do I get rich?", ask "how do people with my income end up broke?". The forward question invites wishful thinking and a search through an infinite space of clever moves. The inverted question produces a short, concrete list within minutes, and most items on it are things you control.

The slogan comes from the nineteenth-century mathematician Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi, credited with the maxim "man muss immer umkehren", one must always invert. Jacobi meant it technically: many results in analysis fall out when the problem is restated in inverse form. Charlie Munger borrowed the phrase and made it a rule of life. His version, repeated in many talks, was that he only wanted to know where he was going to die so that he could never go there. The joke carries the method: you do not need a map of every road to success if you have a reliable map of the cliffs.

Why backward is easier than forward

There is an asymmetry in what minds are good at. Asked for a strategy to win, most people produce vague optimism. Asked how something could go wrong, the same people produce a precise list in seconds: the supplier who is a single point of failure, the assumption nobody tested, the colleague who was never told. Evolution tuned us to spot threats, not to design triumphs. Inversion points that strength at the problem.

There is a second asymmetry, in the problems themselves. In most endeavours there are many ways to fail and few ways to win, and the failures repeat across people and eras while the wins are specific and often lucky. Removing a known cause of failure is therefore more reliable than adding a hoped-for cause of success. Munger called this avoiding stupidity rather than seeking brilliance, and was clear which one had made him money.

Two domains, the same move

Engineering has institutionalised inversion under other names. A failure mode and effects analysis does not ask "how will this part work?" but "in what ways could it fail, how likely is each, and how bad would it be?". A pre-mortem, which the psychologist Gary Klein proposed for project teams, asks the group to imagine that a year has passed and the project has failed badly, then to write the history of that failure. Teams raise objections in a pre-mortem that they would never raise in a planning meeting, because the exercise makes it safe to be the pessimist.

Investing offers the same lesson with a longer record. "Which stock will go up?" has no reliable answer. "What reliably destroys a portfolio?" does: leverage that forces you to sell at the bottom, concentration in a story you cannot verify, fees that compound against you, and trading on news everyone already has. An investor who merely refuses all four has removed most of the ways to lose without having had a single good idea. Warren Buffett's two rules, never lose money and never forget rule one, are inversion dressed as a joke.

Software has its own version. "How do I make this service reliable?" can absorb a month of architecture. "What would page me at three in the morning?" produces the unbounded queue, the retry storm, the config that differs between staging and production, and the dependency with no timeout, and you fix those first.

The failure mode of inversion

Inversion misleads when avoiding the bad becomes the whole plan. A company that only asks what could go wrong never ships, because everything could. A person organised around never being embarrassed avoids every situation in which they might have grown. Removing causes of failure is necessary and underrated, but not sufficient: at some point you still have to do the forward thing, and no list of cliffs tells you where the summit is.

Over-application has a texture. The inverted list has become a veto, every item weighted the same regardless of likelihood, and the meeting ends with nothing decided. The fix is to pair inversion with probability: of the ten ways this could fail, which three carry most of the risk, and what does each cost to remove? A pre-mortem that produces thirty objections and no ranking has not finished. And some forward questions deserve to stay forward: "how do we delight this customer?" is not "how do we avoid annoying them?", and a product built only from the second is merely inoffensive.

A habit for tomorrow

Take one goal you are working toward this week and write down, in five minutes, the three most reliable ways to make sure it does not happen. Be specific: not "poor communication" but "the client learns about the delay from the invoice". Then check whether your current plan already does any of them. It usually does one.

The deeper habit is to notice when you are stuck on a forward question and ask whether its inverse is easier. It often is, because the inverse is a question about how the world already fails, and you have been watching that your whole life.

Connections

Inversion is the natural partner of the circle of competence from yesterday: you rarely know enough to say what will work in an unfamiliar domain, but you can almost always say what has failed there before, so inversion is the first tool to reach for at the edge of the circle. It also guards against the man-with-a-hammer problem from the first lesson, since "how would this plan fail?" is one of the few questions that reliably surfaces the models you forgot to apply. Later in the series the pre-mortem returns as the defence against the planning fallacy, and Munger's list of psychological tendencies is itself an inverted project: a catalogue of the standard ways a mind goes wrong, compiled so you can check your own against it.

Keep

  • When a forward question is hard, ask its inverse: what would guarantee failure? The list is shorter, more concrete and mostly within your control.
  • Avoiding the standard causes of failure is more reliable than finding a cause of success, because failures repeat across people and eras while wins are specific.
  • Run a pre-mortem on any plan that matters: assume it has failed, write the history, then rank the causes by likelihood and cost to remove.
  • Inversion clears the ground but does not build on it; pair it with a forward question and with probabilities, or it turns into a veto that stops everything.
  • At the edge of your competence, inversion is the first tool to reach for, because you can name past failures in a domain long before you can predict its successes.

Names worth knowing

  • Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi — German mathematician (1804–1851), credited with the maxim "man muss immer umkehren": many problems in analysis become tractable when restated in inverse form.
  • Charlie Munger — Berkshire Hathaway's vice-chairman, who turned Jacobi's maxim into a rule of life and argued that avoiding standard stupidities beats seeking brilliance.
  • Gary Klein — Research psychologist who proposed the pre-mortem: imagine the project has already failed, then explain why.
