The first tools

Oldowan choppers, Acheulean hand-axes, and a million years of one design

4 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

About 2.6 million years ago, in East Africa, some small-brained ancestor of ours struck one stone against another and kept the sharp flake that came off. With it a creature with the teeth of an ape could open a zebra. The second great design, the hand-axe, followed a million years later and then stayed almost exactly the same for a million more, which is the strangest fact in the early human record and one of the most telling.

In 1931 Louis Leakey went to Olduvai Gorge, a dry ravine cut through the plain of northern Tanzania, looking for stone tools, and on his first day there he found one. He and Mary Leakey worked the gorge for decades, and the crude choppers and flakes they dug from its lowest beds gave their name to the oldest industry people have: Oldowan. Older tools have since turned up at Gona in Ethiopia, about 2.6 million years old, and at Lomekwi in Kenya a scatter of battered stones dated to 3.3 million years is argued over still, because it comes from a time before anything called Homo existed. Whoever the first toolmakers were, they had a brain less than half the size of a modern one and understood something exact about rocks.

What the stone does

Flint, chert, obsidian and fine basalt all break the same way. Strike one hard near its edge, with the blow angled in toward the body of the stone, and the shock runs through the rock as a cone, the way a pellet leaves a cone-shaped chip in a pane of glass. A flake springs off along the edge of that cone, and its edge is sharper than any knife a smith will make for the next two and a half million years, because it is simply where the stone parted. The maker, holding a fist-sized cobble in one hand and a harder, rounder hammerstone in the other, takes off a flake, turns the cobble, takes off another beside the first scar, and after a few blows holds a jagged-edged chopper and a small pile of razors. Archaeologists now suspect the flakes were the real product and the chopper mostly the leftover.

It is easy to describe and surprisingly hard to do. Students who try it spend an afternoon bruising their thumbs before the first clean flake comes off, and the first toolmakers did it well enough that their flakes still show the practised angle.

What the flake was for

The answer is on the bones. At sites of the same age the long bones of antelope and zebra carry fine parallel cut marks where a flake has sliced meat away, and the shafts have been smashed open with a stone for the marrow inside. A hominin cannot bite through a hide or crack a femur with its teeth. A hyena can do the second and a lion the first, and until the flake those animals had the carcass and the hominins had what was left. Marrow is the richest food on the savanna, and a creature that could get at it had found a store of calories its body alone could never have reached. Over the next million years the brain, which burns a fifth of a resting human's energy, grew from around six hundred cubic centimetres toward nine hundred and more. Whether the richer diet caused the growth or merely allowed it is argued; that the two went together is not.

The hand-axe

About 1.76 million years ago, at Kokiselei on the western shore of Lake Turkana, a different kind of tool appears, and soon afterwards it is everywhere in Africa. It is shaped like a teardrop or an almond, worked on both faces so that one sharp edge runs all the way round, and it comes to a point. The nineteenth century named it after Saint-Acheul, a suburb of Amiens where gravel diggers kept turning them up, and the name Acheulean has stuck. Making one takes dozens of planned blows, and the finer late examples were thinned with a soft hammer of antler or wood that lifts a long shallow flake without shattering the piece. A skilled knapper today needs an hour for a good one and years to become skilled.

The chopper was whatever remained when the flakes had been taken. The hand-axe is something else: a shape the maker carried in the head before the first blow and imposed on the stone through a sequence that had to be followed in order. Many are more symmetrical than any job demands. Wear on the edges shows butchery, digging and the working of wood, and what the point was for is not settled. Whatever its uses, the hand-axe is the first object in the record that someone made to look a certain way.

A million years of the same tool

For a very long time after that, almost nothing changed. A hand-axe from 1.5 million years ago and one from 300,000 years ago, dug up in South Africa, India or the gravel of the Thames, are the same tool, made the same way. Across more than a million years and several species of Homo, the design was handed on faithfully and improved hardly at all. There is nothing like it later in the story. The bronze sword changed in a few centuries and the rifle in a few decades; the hand-axe outlasted the species that invented it.

The stillness says something about the minds involved. A hand-axe cannot be learned by watching once; it has to be taught, probably over years, and the knowledge had to survive its teacher. Early Homo could manage that. What they seem not to have managed was the next step, taking a good design and making it better and passing the better one on, so that improvements piled up from one generation to the next. Every maker learned the shape, got about as good as the model, and had no way to hand down a refinement that was hard to see. A fire can be kept by following a sequence of instructions; a better hand-axe needs a word for "thinner here", and the strongest explanation on offer is that the hand-axe makers lacked the language to say it.

For the moment there was no money, because a band of a few dozen owned nothing worth storing and met nobody to trade with. Force was personal, a matter of fists and thrown stones, and the hand-axe, which makes a poor weapon, shows no sign of ever having been adapted into one. What had changed was diet, and with it, slowly, the brain.
