The first tools

Oldowan choppers, Acheulean hand-axes, and a million years of one design

4 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

Around 2.6 million years ago hominins in East Africa learned to strike one stone against another and get a sharp edge. The trick, controlled fracture, gave them access to meat and marrow their teeth could not reach, and that calorie surplus is the first move in the energy thread. The second design, the hand-axe, then stayed essentially the same for more than a million years, which says as much about early minds as the tool does.

Fine-grained stone such as flint, chert, quartzite and basalt breaks in a predictable way. Hit it hard near an edge, at an angle of less than ninety degrees to the surface, and the force spreads through the rock in a cone, like the mark a pellet leaves in a window. A flake pops off along the edge of the cone, and that edge is sharper than any steel knife, often only a few atoms across at the tip. The process is called conchoidal fracture, after the shell-like ripples it leaves on the flake, and every stone tool for the next two and a half million years is a way of controlling it.

The oldest widely accepted tools made this way come from Gona in Ethiopia and are about 2.6 million years old. Older and cruder flaked stones from Lomekwi in Kenya have been dated to 3.3 million years, before the genus Homo existed, and are still argued over. In either case the first toolmakers were small-brained hominins who nonetheless understood something precise about how rocks behave.

The Oldowan: a core and its flakes

The first industry is named Oldowan after Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania, where Mary and Louis Leakey excavated it from the 1930s onward. The method is simple to describe and hard to do. The maker holds a fist-sized cobble, the core, in one hand and a harder, rounder hammerstone in the other, and strikes the core near its edge. A flake detaches, and another blow next to the scar brings off a second. After a few blows the core has a jagged edge and can be used as a chopper, and the flakes, which many archaeologists now think were the real product, can slice hide, cut meat from bone and shave wood.

What the Oldowan unlocked was carcasses. Hominin teeth and nails cannot open a zebra. A sharp flake can, and a hammerstone can crack a long bone for marrow, the fattiest food on the savanna and one that other scavengers could not reach without a hyena's jaws. Cut-marked animal bones appear in the record alongside the first tools. The tool was a way to borrow calories that the body could not otherwise collect, an energy technology before fire, which is where the next chapter goes.

That surplus had consequences. Brains are expensive, taking about a fifth of a resting human's energy, and the fossil record shows brain size climbing from roughly 600 cubic centimetres in early Homo toward 900 and more in Homo erectus over the following million years. One well-known hypothesis holds that richer food allowed a smaller gut and freed energy for a bigger brain. The causation is debated. The correlation between better tools, more meat and larger brains is not.

The Acheulean: the hand-axe and the idea of a shape

Around 1.76 million years ago, at Kokiselei in Kenya and soon across Africa, a new tool appeared. It is named Acheulean after Saint-Acheul, a suburb of Amiens in France where it was first recognised in the nineteenth century: a teardrop or almond-shaped stone, flaked on both faces, with a continuous edge all the way round and a point at one end. Making one takes dozens of deliberate removals and, in later examples, a soft hammer of antler or wood to thin the piece without shattering it. Experiments put the work at an hour or more for a skilled knapper, and the skill itself at years of practice.

The hand-axe differs in kind from a chopper. A chopper is whatever remains when enough flakes have been taken. A hand-axe is a shape that existed in the maker's head before the first blow and was imposed on the stone through a planned sequence. The symmetry is often better than the job required. Wear traces support butchery, digging and woodworking, and whatever it was used for, it is the first object in the record that was made to look a particular way.

After that the design barely moved. Hand-axes from 1.5 million years ago and from 300,000 years ago are recognisably the same tool, made the same way, from South Africa to India to Britain. For more than a million years, across several species of Homo, the design was transmitted faithfully and improved hardly at all. The previous chapter proposed the interval between tool improvements as the best single measure of how fast a society learns. By that measure, the Acheulean world was learning very slowly.

What the stasis says about information

The slowness is the most important fact in this chapter, because it points at the information thread. A hand-axe cannot be made after watching once. It has to be taught, probably by demonstration over years, and the knowledge has to survive the death of the teacher. Early Homo could clearly do that much. What they apparently could not do was build on it: take a good design, make it better, and pass the better one on, so that improvements accumulate. That ratchet, in which each generation starts from the last one's best, is what later chapters will call cumulative culture, and it depends on something the hand-axe makers seem to have lacked. The strongest candidate is full language, the subject of chapter four.

The other threads barely move yet. There is no money, because there is nothing to store and nobody to trade with beyond the band. Force is personal and immediate; a hand-axe makes a poor weapon, and there is little sign the design was ever adjusted to be one. Energy has shifted modestly, through diet. Tools and information are the live threads, and they are tied together, since the tool is only as good as the method for passing it on.
