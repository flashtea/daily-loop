History of Civilization · Chapter 10 · The agricultural revolution

The end of the Ice Age and the Fertile Crescent

Climate, wild grasses and the Natufians

21 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

Between about 19,000 and 11,700 years ago the planet warmed out of the last glacial maximum, carbon dioxide rose, and the hills between the Mediterranean and the Zagros filled with dense stands of wild wheat and barley. The people living there learned to harvest the grain in bulk, grind it, store it and stay beside it, two or three thousand years before anyone sowed a seed on purpose. This chapter explains why that climate grew those plants, how much food a stand held, and what the villages of the Natufians looked like.

Between about nineteen thousand and eleven thousand years ago the planet warmed out of the last glacial maximum, and in one arc of hills between the Mediterranean and the Zagros the warming changed what grew. The change belongs to the energy thread. Wild wheat and barley pack a winter's sunlight into a seed that keeps for years, and the people on those hills learned to harvest it in bulk, store it and stay beside it, two or three thousand years before anyone farmed.

The ice goes

At the glacial maximum, roughly 26,000 to 19,000 years ago, ice sheets up to three kilometres thick covered Scandinavia and most of Canada, and the sea stood about 120 metres lower than today. Summer sunlight in the north then strengthened as the Earth's orbit and tilt shifted, and the Greenland ice cores drilled in the early 1990s record what followed: a sharp warming about 14,700 years ago, a relapse into near-glacial cold about 12,900 years ago called the Younger Dryas, and a final warming about 11,700 years ago that opened the Holocene.

Carbon dioxide, measured in air bubbles trapped in Antarctic ice, rose over the same period from about 190 parts per million to about 265, and the plant physiologist Rowan Sage argued in 1995 that glacial air alone had kept wild cereal yields too low to live on. The eastern Mediterranean also settled into the regime it still has, wet winters and a rainless summer of four or five months, and that pattern decides which plants win.

The grass that stored summer

An annual grass in a winter-rain climate germinates with the autumn rains, grows through the mild winter, flowers in spring and dies in the summer drought. Because the plant dies, it puts its reserves into seed, and a large seed gives the next seedling a head start when the rains return. The result is a family of fat, starchy seeds that ripen within a few weeks of each other and then keep: dry grain holds about 3,300 kilocalories a kilogram and stores for years if kept from damp and mice. The founder crops of the region all fit the pattern, wild emmer and einkorn wheat, wild barley, lentil, pea, chickpea, bitter vetch and flax, growing in dense stands on the hills of the Levant and southeastern Anatolia.

How much food such a stand holds was measured in 1966. Jack Harlan, an agronomist at the University of Oklahoma, went to the slopes of Karacadağ in southeastern Turkey when the wild einkorn was ripe and harvested it by hand and with a sickle he knapped from flint. Stripping heads by hand he gathered about two kilograms of spikelets an hour, roughly one kilogram of clean grain, and calculated that a family working for three weeks could bring in more grain than it could eat in a year, around a metric ton; he published the figures in Archaeology in 1967. The ripe stands shatter, though: the rachis that holds each spikelet to the stalk turns brittle and a day of wind drops the grain, so a harvester has about a week per stand and a reason to be there when it opens.

The people who stayed

Dorothy Garrod named the culture that lived on these hills the Natufian, after Wadi an-Natuf, where she dug Shuqba cave in 1928. It runs from about 14,500 to 11,500 years ago across the Levant. Its signature tools are lunates, small crescent-shaped flint blades mounted in rows as arrow barbs or sickle teeth, and many sickle blades carry a glossy sheen that forms when flint cuts grass stems for long enough. The sites also hold heavy stone mortars and grinding slabs, some cut into the bedrock itself.

Ain Mallaha, beside the former Hula lake in the upper Jordan valley, was excavated by Jean Perrot from 1955 and is the clearest case of a Natufian village. Its round houses were dug half a metre into the slope, walled with stone, and ranged from three to nine metres across, with hearths and storage pits inside. Several dozen people lived there through every season and hunted mountain gazelle above everything else. Among about a hundred burials one holds an elderly person laid with a hand on a puppy, around 12,000 years old. Cemeteries are new in this period; a band that moves on does not accumulate a hundred graves in one place.

At Shubayqa 1 in northeastern Jordan, Amaia Arranz-Otaegui's team identified charred crumbs of flatbread in two fireplaces dated to about 14,400 years ago, made from wild einkorn and barley flour and the tubers of club-rush, several thousand years before any domesticated cereal. Ohalo II on the Sea of Galilee, dug by Dani Nadel after the lake fell in 1989, pushes grain gathering back to about 23,000 years ago, and whether its people were already sowing is disputed.

What the cold did

The Younger Dryas hit the Natufians in the middle of their run, and its cause is still argued; Wallace Broecker proposed in the 1980s that a surge of meltwater from the North American ice sheet shut down the Atlantic circulation that carries heat north, and most climate scientists accept some version of that. Late Natufian sites are smaller and their people more mobile than before, and Ofer Bar-Yosef argued that the dry centuries pushed some groups toward sowing the grain they had been gathering. Lisa Maher and Tobias Richter have answered that the sites do not line up neatly with the climate curve and that cultivation was already under way. Both readings agree on the sequence: people lived beside wild cereal stands, stored the harvest and buried their dead in one place for two thousand years before anyone domesticated a plant.

Staying put forced things in the other threads. A stored harvest can be measured, shared, hoarded and stolen, so it created the first property worth defending and the first reason to build a house that could be shut. The next chapter follows the harvest into the ground: how sowing from a store of seed, repeated for centuries, turned a shattering wild grass into one that waited for the sickle.
