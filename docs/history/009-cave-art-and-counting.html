Cave art and counting

Symbolic thought, tally sticks and the roots of record-keeping

19 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

Late in the Ice Age people began leaving marks outside their own heads: animals painted on cave walls by lamplight, and rows of notches cut into bone. The painting needed pigment chemistry, fat-burning lamps and scaffolding; the notches needed the idea that one mark can stand for one thing. The second idea is the root of every ledger, tax roll and account that the rest of this series will depend on.

The information thread of this series has so far run through speech, which leaves nothing behind. Somewhere after 70,000 years ago people began to put marks on ochre, bone and cave walls that carried meaning to another person, or to the same person a month later. The pictures show that the makers could hold an image of an absent animal in mind and reproduce it; the notches show that they could make a mark stand for a count. The caves are famous and the notches are obscure, and the notches are the ones that led to accounting.

Pigment, fat and light

Red and yellow came from ochre, iron oxides dug from outcrops and ground on stone palettes; black came from charcoal or manganese dioxide. Heating yellow ochre to roughly 250 to 300 degrees drives off water and turns it red, and the painters at Lascaux used that to widen their range. The powder was mixed with water or animal fat and put on the wall with fingers, pads of fur and chewed twigs. At Blombos Cave on the South African coast Christopher Henshilwood's team found two abalone shells, about 100,000 years old, in which ochre had been ground with bone and charcoal into a paste.

The deep galleries at Lascaux are hundreds of metres from daylight. The painters burned animal fat in shallow stone lamps with a wick of juniper or moss; more than a hundred have been recovered there, and one carved from red sandstone, found by André Glory in 1960, still held juniper residue in its bowl. Such a lamp gives about the light of a candle and burns for an hour on a handful of fat. Sockets cut in the rock below the Hall of the Bulls show where beams were wedged to hold a scaffold.

What the caves hold

The oldest drawing anyone has found is a red ochre crosshatch on a stone flake at Blombos, published by Henshilwood in 2018 and about 73,000 years old. On Sulawesi in Indonesia, Adam Brumm and Maxime Aubert's team dated a painted warty pig at Leang Tedongnge to at least 45,500 years in 2021, using uranium-thorium decay in the calcite over the paint, and in 2024 a scene of human-like figures with a pig at Leang Karampuang came out at about 51,200 years. The oldest figurative art known is therefore in Southeast Asia, which fits the migration route of the sixth chapter.

Chauvet, in the Ardèche gorge, was found on 18 December 1994 by Jean-Marie Chauvet, Éliette Brunel and Christian Hillaire, who felt a draught from a cleft in the rock; its lions and rhinoceroses were drawn in charcoal around 36,000 years ago. Lascaux was found on 12 September 1940 by four teenagers from Montignac whose dog had gone down a hole left by a fallen pine; it holds about 600 painted animals made around 17,000 years ago. What the paintings were for is unknown, and the honest position is to leave it so. Two facts constrain any theory: the animals painted are mostly not the animals eaten, since reindeer bones fill the floor at Lascaux and reindeer barely appear on its walls; and the same small set of abstract signs recurs across the continent, 32 of them by Genevieve von Petzinger's count from 370 European sites.

One notch for one thing

The counting objects are plainer and older than most of the paintings. The Lebombo bone is a baboon fibula from Border Cave, on the ridge between Eswatini and South Africa, excavated by Peter Beaumont in 1973 and dated to about 43,000 years; it carries 29 notches, and Francesco d'Errico's microscope study in 2012 found that they had been cut with different tools at different times, which is what a tally looks like and what decoration does not. A second baboon fibula, about 10 centimetres long with a sliver of quartz fixed in one end, was found in 1950 by the Belgian geologist Jean de Heinzelin at Ishango, where the Semliki river leaves Lake Edward in Congo; it is about 20,000 years old. Its notches run in three columns of groups, one reading 11, 13, 17, 19, another 3, 6, 4, 8, 10, 5, 5, 7. De Heinzelin saw an arithmetic game in the doublings and the primes; Alexander Marshack read it in 1972 as a six-month lunar record; most archaeologists now think the groups are a tally of something unknown and the primes a coincidence.

The mechanism of a tally is simple enough to be easy to underrate. A person can see at a glance that there are three or four things without counting; beyond that the eye fails and the hand takes over, one cut for each item, so that two quantities can be compared without any name for the number. Grouping the cuts, five then a gap, lets a line of marks be read back quickly. A tally is a memory that does not fade, that can be carried, and that can be checked by someone who was absent when it was made, and that last property is what the chapters on grain, taxation and debt will turn on. The English Exchequer took its tax receipts on split hazel tallies until 1826, and on 16 October 1834 the clerks burning the stored sticks under the House of Lords set fire to the Palace of Westminster.

Whether some of these marks were calendars is the live argument. Marshack claimed lunar notation for a bone plaque from Abri Blanchard with 69 marks in a winding line; d'Errico showed in 1989 that several of Marshack's objects had been cut in one sitting with one point, though he accepted the case for others. In 2023 Ben Bacon and colleagues proposed that the dots beside painted animals count lunar months to the mating season, and other prehistorians answered that the sequences are too short to carry it. People who tracked herds through the year had the motive and the means to record the moon, and no single object yet proves they did.

The band that painted Lascaux hunted with dogs, bows and sewn clothing, and kept its counts on bone. Within a few thousand years the ice would retreat, wild grasses would thicken on the hills of the Levant, and the next chapter follows the people who stayed put to harvest them.
