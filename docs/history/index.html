Part I — Before civilization

  1. Ch. 1
    Why study the whole arc Five threads from the first stone tool to the first software agent

    History read as a sequence of kings is mostly noise. Read as the record of how humans captured energy, built tools, stored information, organised money and projected force, it becomes one compounding process with a few rules that still hold. This chapter sets out those five threads and the lens the series will use.

    03 Oct
  2. Ch. 2
    The first tools Oldowan choppers, Acheulean hand-axes, and a million years of one design

    Around 2.6 million years ago hominins in East Africa learned to strike one stone against another and get a sharp edge. The trick, controlled fracture, gave them access to meat and marrow their teeth could not reach, and that calorie surplus is the first move in the energy thread. The second design, the hand-axe, then stayed essentially the same for more than a million years, which says as much about early minds as the tool does.

    04 Oct
  3. Ch. 3
    Fire Cooked food, the bigger brain, the longer day, and the first energy technology

    Stone tools let hominins borrow calories from carcasses. Fire let them borrow calories from chemistry, by doing part of digestion outside the body. Controlled fire is the first time humans captured an energy source other than their own muscles, and its consequences run through every thread in this series: a smaller gut and a larger brain, a day that did not end at dusk, a hearth that made a group, and eventually the kilns and furnaces that turn clay and ore into civilization.

    05 Oct
  4. Ch. 4
    Language The information technology that made cumulative culture possible

    Tools gave hominins leverage over matter and fire gave them leverage over energy. Language gave them leverage over each other's minds: a way to move a thought from one head into another cheaply, precisely, and about things not present. It is the first information technology, and it is the reason human culture accumulates instead of resetting every generation.

    07 Oct
  5. Ch. 5
    The hunter-gatherer economy Band size, Dunbar's number, egalitarianism, mobility, and why wealth could not accumulate

    For more than 95 percent of the species' existence, every human lived in a small mobile band that owned almost nothing, shared almost everything and tolerated no boss. This was an economy tuned to its energy source, wild food that spoils, moves and cannot be stored, rather than a failure to invent property or hierarchy. Understanding why wealth could not accumulate in that world explains why everything changed the moment it could.

    11 Oct
  6. Ch. 6
    Out of Africa Migration, the peopling of the continents, and the Neanderthal encounter

    Between about 70,000 and 15,000 years ago a tropical ape that had spent its whole existence in Africa reached every habitable continent, crossed open sea, lived through an ice age and replaced every other kind of human. The capability behind it was a package: fire, sewn clothing, projectile weapons, boats, and knowledge carried by language through wide social networks. The result is a species that adapts by culture rather than by body.
