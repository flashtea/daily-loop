History of Civilization

From the first stone tools to the present, told through five threads: energy, tools, information, money, and force. Less detail than Durant, more technology than a textbook.

Part I — Before civilization

  1. Ch. 1
    Why study the whole arc Five threads from the first stone tool to the first software agent

    History read as a sequence of kings is mostly noise. Read as the record of how humans captured energy, built tools, stored information, organised money and projected force, it becomes one compounding process with a few rules that still hold. This chapter sets out those five threads and the lens the series will use.

    03 Oct
  2. Ch. 2
    The first tools Oldowan choppers, Acheulean hand-axes, and a million years of one design

    Around 2.6 million years ago hominins in East Africa learned to strike one stone against another and get a sharp edge. The trick, controlled fracture, gave them access to meat and marrow their teeth could not reach, and that calorie surplus is the first move in the energy thread. The second design, the hand-axe, then stayed essentially the same for more than a million years, which says as much about early minds as the tool does.

    04 Oct
  3. Ch. 3
    Fire Cooked food, the bigger brain, the longer day, and the first energy technology

    Stone tools let hominins borrow calories from carcasses. Fire let them borrow calories from chemistry, by doing part of digestion outside the body. Controlled fire is the first time humans captured an energy source other than their own muscles, and its consequences run through every thread in this series: a smaller gut and a larger brain, a day that did not end at dusk, a hearth that made a group, and eventually the kilns and furnaces that turn clay and ore into civilization.

    05 Oct
  4. Ch. 4
    Language The information technology that made cumulative culture possible

    Tools gave hominins leverage over matter and fire gave them leverage over energy. Language gave them leverage over each other's minds: a way to move a thought from one head into another cheaply, precisely, and about things not present. It is the first information technology, and it is the reason human culture accumulates instead of resetting every generation.

    07 Oct
  5. Ch. 5
    The hunter-gatherer economy Band size, Dunbar's number, egalitarianism, mobility, and why wealth could not accumulate

    For more than 95 percent of the species' existence, every human lived in a small mobile band that owned almost nothing, shared almost everything and tolerated no boss. This was an economy tuned to its energy source, wild food that spoils, moves and cannot be stored, rather than a failure to invent property or hierarchy. Understanding why wealth could not accumulate in that world explains why everything changed the moment it could.

    11 Oct
  6. Ch. 6
    Out of Africa Migration, the peopling of the continents, and the Neanderthal encounter

    Between about 70,000 and 15,000 years ago a tropical ape that had spent its whole existence in Africa reached every habitable continent, crossed open sea, lived through an ice age and replaced every other kind of human. The capability behind it was a package: fire, sewn clothing, projectile weapons, boats, and knowledge carried by language through wide social networks. The result is a species that adapts by culture rather than by body.

    13 Oct
  7. Ch. 7
    The bow, the needle, the boat The Upper Paleolithic toolkit and the first long-distance trade

    Between about 70,000 and 12,000 years ago the toolkit changed faster than it had in the previous two million. Three devices carry the story: the bow, which stored muscle energy and released it in a few milliseconds; the eyed needle, which turned hides into fitted clothing; and the boat, which turned coasts and islands into territory. With them came the first objects that travelled hundreds of kilometres from their source, and the first things that look like money.

    15 Oct
  8. Ch. 8
    The dog and the first domestication The beginning of humans engineering other species

    The first species humans changed was the wolf, and the change came before farming, pottery or any village. A dog is a wolf bred, over hundreds of generations, for willingness to live near people, and the side effects of that one selection, from floppy ears to a gut that digests starch, show what engineering a species does. The method, keep the tame and discard the rest, is the one that later produced sheep, wheat and maize.

    17 Oct
  9. Ch. 9
    Cave art and counting Symbolic thought, tally sticks and the roots of record-keeping

    Late in the Ice Age people began leaving marks outside their own heads: animals painted on cave walls by lamplight, and rows of notches cut into bone. The painting needed pigment chemistry, fat-burning lamps and scaffolding; the notches needed the idea that one mark can stand for one thing. The second idea is the root of every ledger, tax roll and account that the rest of this series will depend on.

Part II — The agricultural revolution

  1. Ch. 10
    The end of the Ice Age and the Fertile Crescent Climate, wild grasses and the Natufians

    Between about 19,000 and 11,700 years ago the planet warmed out of the last glacial maximum, carbon dioxide rose, and the hills between the Mediterranean and the Zagros filled with dense stands of wild wheat and barley. The people living there learned to harvest the grain in bulk, grind it, store it and stay beside it, two or three thousand years before anyone sowed a seed on purpose. This chapter explains why that climate grew those plants, how much food a stand held, and what the villages of the Natufians looked like.

    21 Oct
  2. Ch. 11
    Domesticating plants Wheat, barley, rice, maize, and why some regions got lucky

    Between about 11,500 and 6,000 years ago, people in at least four separate regions turned wild grasses into plants that could no longer survive without them. Nobody planned it; the arithmetic of harvesting with a sickle and sowing from the basket did the selecting over centuries. This chapter explains what changed in the plant, how fast it changed, and why the Fertile Crescent had the easiest raw material.

    23 Oct
  3. Ch. 12
    Domesticating animals Sheep, goats, cattle and pigs; milk, wool, traction, and disease

    Between about 10,500 and 8,000 years ago villagers in the arc from southeastern Anatolia to the Zagros took the mouflon, the bezoar goat, the aurochs and the wild boar into their herds. A ruminant turns grass, which a human stomach cannot digest, into meat, milk, fibre and pulling power, and a herd stores that energy on the hoof. This chapter explains how archaeologists see herding in a heap of bones, what the animals became, and what milk, wool, the ox and the new diseases did to the people who kept them.

    25 Oct
