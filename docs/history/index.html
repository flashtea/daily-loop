Part I — Before civilization

  1. Ch. 1
    Why study the whole arc Five threads from the first stone tool to the present

    History read as a parade of kings is mostly noise. Read as the story of how people learned to capture energy, make tools, keep and move knowledge, trade with strangers and fight, it becomes one long process with a few rules that still hold. This opening chapter lays out those five threads and the way the chapters that follow will use them.

  2. Ch. 2
    The first tools Oldowan choppers, Acheulean hand-axes, and a million years of one design

    About 2.6 million years ago, in East Africa, some small-brained ancestor of ours struck one stone against another and kept the sharp flake that came off. With it a creature with the teeth of an ape could open a zebra. The second great design, the hand-axe, followed a million years later and then stayed almost exactly the same for a million more, which is the strangest fact in the early human record and one of the most telling.

    04 Oct
  3. Ch. 3
    Fire Cooked food, the bigger brain, the longer day, and the first energy technology

    With fire, people began to borrow energy from something other than their own bodies. A cooked root or a roasted joint gives up far more of itself than the raw thing, and a lineage that cooked could afford a smaller gut and a larger brain. Fire also lit the evening, gathered the band around one spot, and put the first heat into the hands of the toolmaker, from the hardened spear point to the kiln.

    05 Oct
  4. Ch. 4
    Language The information technology that made cumulative culture possible

    Monkeys have calls and bees have dances, and none of them can say that the leopard seen by the river yesterday is gone and will be back tomorrow. Human language can, and the ability to talk about what is absent, past and imaginary is what let knowledge pile up from one generation to the next instead of dying with the people who held it. It left no fossils, so the story of when and why it arose has to be read sideways, from bones, genes, beads and the habits of living apes.

    07 Oct
  5. Ch. 5
    The hunter-gatherer economy Band size, Dunbar's number, egalitarianism, mobility, and why wealth could not accumulate

    For more than nine-tenths of the time there have been people, every one of them lived in a small wandering band that owned almost nothing, shared nearly everything and tolerated no boss. That was an economy fitted to wild food, which spoils and moves and cannot be piled up. Understanding why nobody could get rich in that world explains why everything changed the moment somebody could.

    11 Oct
  6. Ch. 6
    Out of Africa Migration, the peopling of the continents, and the Neanderthal encounter

    A hundred thousand years ago modern humans lived only in Africa, and the rest of the Old World belonged to other kinds of people who had been adapting to its cold for hundreds of thousands of years. Within fifty thousand years the Africans had reached every habitable continent, crossed open sea and lived through an ice age, and they were the only humans left. They did it without changing their bodies, by carrying their adaptations in their hands and in their heads.

    13 Oct
  7. Ch. 7
    The bow, the needle, the boat The Upper Paleolithic toolkit and the first long-distance trade

    In the last fifty thousand years of the Ice Age the toolkit changed more than it had in the previous two million. A hunter at the end of it carried a bow, wore sewn clothes, and might have been born on an island his grandparents reached by boat, and the shells on his necklace had come from a sea he had never seen. With those shells, passed from hand to hand across hundreds of kilometres, something that looks very like money enters the story.
