History of Civilization · Chapter 4 · Before civilization

Language

The information technology that made cumulative culture possible

7 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

Monkeys have calls and bees have dances, and none of them can say that the leopard seen by the river yesterday is gone and will be back tomorrow. Human language can, and the ability to talk about what is absent, past and imaginary is what let knowledge pile up from one generation to the next instead of dying with the people who held it. It left no fossils, so the story of when and why it arose has to be read sideways, from bones, genes, beads and the habits of living apes.

Vervet monkeys in East Africa have three alarm calls, one each for eagle, leopard and snake, and a troop that hears the eagle call looks up while a troop that hears the leopard call runs for the trees. Honeybees returning to the hive dance the direction and distance of a patch of flowers. All of this is communication, and none of it can produce the sentence "the leopard that was by the river yesterday has gone, but there will be another one tomorrow, so take the other path", which in twenty words manages an absent animal, a past, a negation, a prediction and a plan. Only people can say such things, and a creature that can say them can teach, bargain, plot and lie.

What makes it different

Human speech is built from a few dozen meaningless sounds, which combine into tens of thousands of words, which combine by rule into sentences without end. Language can say what has never been said, and can say it about things that are not here and not now, including things that do not exist at all, which is the property that makes planning and storytelling and religion possible. And any child, exposed to any human language, picks it up without being taught, by about the age of four.

The body was reshaped for it, as it had been reshaped for cooking. The human larynx sits lower in the throat than any other ape's, which makes humans the only primate that can choke on its food and gives the voice two resonating chambers and a wide range of vowels. The canal that carries the nerve to the tongue is wider in modern people, and in Neanderthals, than in earlier humans. A gene called FOXP2, which differs from the chimpanzee version by two amino acids, is involved in the motor control of speech, and people who inherit a damaged copy struggle to string sounds together and to apply the rules of grammar. There is no single gene for language. Together these are the marks of an animal that had been selected for talking for a very long time.

When

Speech leaves no fossils, so the dating is roundabout. The anatomy, including a hyoid bone of modern form and an ear tuned to the frequencies of speech, is in place in Neanderthals and early Homo sapiens by around 300,000 years ago, so some kind of speech is probably at least that old. What can be dated is behaviour that is hard to imagine without language. At Blombos Cave on the southern coast of South Africa, about 75,000 years ago, people engraved cross-hatched patterns into lumps of red ochre and drilled small sea-snail shells to string as beads, which is to say they were making marks that stood for something and ornaments that said who they were. After about 50,000 years ago art, music and a flood of new tools appear. The cautious reading is that fully modern language existed by 100,000 years ago and was everywhere by 50,000.

Why

Robin Dunbar's account is the most useful one on offer. Monkeys and apes hold their groups together by grooming, which builds trust one pair at a time and costs hours a day, so the size of a group is capped by the time its members can spare for each other's fur. Across the primates the size of the neocortex predicts the size of the group, and the human brain predicts a group of about 150, which is far too many to groom. Language, in Dunbar's view, is grooming at a distance and in bulk: one speaker can reach several listeners at once, and what they trade is mostly gossip, which is to say information about who can be trusted.

Whatever first drove it, the great payoff was teaching. A chimpanzee mother does not teach her infant to crack nuts; the infant watches for years and mostly works it out alone. A human parent can say "not that stone, this one, and hold it like this", and an afternoon's instruction replaces years of trial and error.

The ratchet

Michael Tomasello, a psychologist who spent a career comparing children with apes, calls the result the ratchet effect. An improvement, once made, is passed on faithfully enough that the next generation starts from it rather than rediscovering it, so that improvements stack. Chimpanzees have local traditions, in nut-cracking and in grooming, and the traditions do not stack. Human culture ratchets, and language is the pawl that keeps it from slipping back.

The hand-axe looks different in that light. A million years of the same shape is what a technology looks like without a ratchet: each maker learned by watching, got about as good as the model, and had no way to pass on a refinement that could not be seen. The acceleration after 50,000 years ago is what one would expect once good ideas stopped dying with the people who had them.

What it changed

Language rearranged the band. A hunt could be planned and roles assigned, so that a dozen people drove a herd toward a cliff instead of each chasing an animal alone. Reputation became a kind of wealth: a man known to share could call on others in a lean season, which is the seed of credit. Kinship could be named across generations and between bands, which made alliances possible, and with them the exchange of shells and good stone over hundreds of kilometres. The stories told at the fire became a band's memory of waterholes and routes and dangers, an archive kept alive by retelling, tens of thousands of years before anyone pressed a reed into clay.

Speech is cheap to make and cheap to lose. It carries as far as a voice and lasts as long as a memory, and everything a band knew lived in its living members, so that one bad decade could wipe out a skill. The remedy for that, writing, was still some ninety thousand years off.
