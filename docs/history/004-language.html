History of Civilization · Chapter 4 · Before civilization

Language

The information technology that made cumulative culture possible

7 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

Tools gave hominins leverage over matter and fire gave them leverage over energy. Language gave them leverage over each other's minds: a way to move a thought from one head into another cheaply, precisely, and about things not present. It is the first information technology, and it is the reason human culture accumulates instead of resetting every generation.

Vervet monkeys have distinct alarm calls for eagles, leopards and snakes. Bees dance the direction and distance of flowers. Chimpanzees use several dozen gestures with stable meanings. None of these systems can produce the sentence "the leopard that was by the river yesterday is not there now, but there will be one tomorrow, so take the other path", which refers to an absent animal, a past, a negation, a future and a plan in fourteen words. Only humans can say it, and the ability to say it separates a clever ape from a species that builds civilizations.

The information thread of this series starts here, at least a hundred thousand years before writing. Writing stores language outside the body, and language is what produced something worth storing.

How it works

Human language rests on a small number of properties that, combined, produce something no other communication system has.

  • Discrete units. Speech is built from a few dozen meaningless sounds, phonemes, which combine into tens of thousands of meaningful words. A call system has one signal per meaning. A combinatorial system has effectively unlimited meanings from a fixed inventory; linguists call the arrangement duality of patterning.
  • Syntax. Words combine by rules into phrases, and phrases nest inside phrases without limit. "The hunter who saw the lion that killed the goat" has a structure, and the structure carries who did what to whom.
  • Displacement. Language can refer to things that are not here and not now, including things that do not exist, which is what makes planning, teaching, trading and lying possible.
  • Learnability. Any human child exposed to any human language acquires it without instruction by about age four.

The body was reshaped around speaking, as the gut was reshaped around cooking. Humans have a lowered larynx, which makes choking easier and gives the vocal tract two resonating chambers and a far larger range of vowels. They have fine voluntary control of breathing, tongue and lips, and the hypoglossal canal that carries the tongue's nerve is wider in Homo sapiens and in Neanderthals than in earlier hominins. The gene FOXP2, which in humans differs from the chimpanzee version by two amino acids, is involved in the motor control of speech; people who inherit a damaged copy struggle to sequence sounds and to apply grammatical rules. No single one of these is a language gene. Together they show a body that had been selected for talking.

When it happened

Speech leaves no fossils, so the dating is indirect. The anatomy, including the hyoid bone and an ear tuned to speech frequencies, is in place in Neanderthals and early Homo sapiens by around 300,000 years ago, so some form of speech is probably that old. What can be dated is behaviour that seems to require language: engraved ochre and perforated shell beads at Blombos Cave in South Africa by about 75,000 years ago, raw materials exchanged over long distances, burials with grave goods, and the appearance of art, music and specialised tools after roughly 50,000 years ago. The conservative reading is that modern language existed by 100,000 years ago and was fully elaborated by 50,000.

Why it evolved

The anthropologist Robin Dunbar offered the most useful account. Primates hold their groups together by grooming, which builds trust one pair at a time and takes hours a day, so group size is limited by the time available. Across primate species, neocortex size predicts group size, and the human brain predicts a group of about 150, which is too many to groom. Language, in Dunbar's account, is grooming at scale. One speaker reaches several listeners, and what they exchange is gossip, meaning information about who can be trusted. The next chapter takes up the figure of 150.

Whatever the first driver, the pay-off was teaching. A chimpanzee mother does not teach her infant to crack nuts; the infant watches for years and mostly works it out alone. A human parent can say "not that rock, this one, and hold it like this", and an afternoon of instruction replaces years of trial and error.

The ratchet

The psychologist Michael Tomasello calls it the ratchet effect. An improvement, once made, is passed on faithfully enough that the next generation starts from it instead of rediscovering it, so improvements stack. Chimpanzee cultures exist, with local traditions in nut-cracking and grooming, and they do not stack. Human culture does, and language is the pawl that stops the ratchet slipping back.

The previous two chapters read differently in that light. The hand-axe stayed the same for a million years, which is what a technology looks like without a ratchet: each maker learned by watching, got about as good as the model, and had no way to transmit a refinement that was hard to see. Fire-keeping, with its sequences of fuel, airflow and tinder, is far easier to pass on in words. The acceleration after 50,000 years ago is what would be expected once good ideas stopped dying with the people who had them.

What it forced in the other threads

Language reorganised the band. Plans could be made and roles assigned, so a hunt became a coordinated drive, which is force applied through information. Reputation became a currency: a known sharer could draw on others in lean times, which is the seed of credit and the subject of the next chapter. Kinship could be named across generations, which made alliances between bands possible, and with them the long-distance exchange of obsidian and shell in chapter seven. Stories at the fire became a group's memory of waterholes, routes and dangers, an oral database maintained by retelling, tens of thousands of years before any clay tablet.

Language also created something new to fight over. Myths, laws and gods are made of words, and a group that shares them can coordinate at a scale kinship cannot reach. Sumerian temples and Egyptian kings will build on exactly that capacity, and the whole history can be told as a sequence of information technologies, speech, writing, the alphabet, print, each widening the circle of minds that can hold one idea at the same time.

The limits

Speech is cheap to produce and cheap to lose. It travels as far as a voice carries and lasts as long as a memory. Everything a band knew lived in its living members and had to be recited to survive them, so one bad decade could erase a skill. Writing will solve exactly that problem, and it is still about ninety thousand years away.
