History of Civilization · Chapter 4 · Before civilization

Language

The information technology that made cumulative culture possible

7 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

Tools gave hominins leverage over matter and fire gave them leverage over energy. Language gave them leverage over each other's minds: a way to move a thought from one head into another cheaply, precisely and about things not present. It is the first information technology, and it is the reason human culture accumulates instead of resetting every generation.

Every animal communicates. Vervet monkeys have distinct alarm calls for eagles, leopards and snakes; bees dance the direction of flowers; chimpanzees use a few dozen gestures. None of them can say "the leopard that was by the river yesterday is not there now, but there will be one tomorrow, so take the other path". That sentence refers to an absent animal, a past, a negation, a future and a plan. Only humans can produce it, and the ability to produce it is the hinge between a clever ape and a species that builds civilizations.

The information thread of this series starts here, at least a hundred thousand years before writing. Writing stores language outside the body. Language is the invention that made there be something worth storing.

How it works

Human language rests on a small number of tricks that, combined, produce something no other communication system has.

  • Discrete units. Speech is built from a few dozen meaningless sounds, phonemes, which combine into tens of thousands of meaningful words. A call system has one signal per meaning; a combinatorial system has effectively unlimited meanings from a fixed inventory. Linguists call this duality of patterning.
  • Syntax. Words combine by rules into phrases, and phrases nest inside phrases without limit. "The hunter who saw the lion that killed the goat" has a structure, not just a sequence, and the structure carries meaning: who did what to whom.
  • Displacement. Language can refer to things that are not here and not now, including things that do not exist. This is what makes planning, teaching, trading and lying possible.
  • Learnability. Any human child, exposed to any human language, acquires it without instruction by about age four. The hardware is universal; the software is local.

The hardware side is real. Humans have a lowered larynx, which makes choking easier but gives the vocal tract two resonating chambers and a far larger range of vowels. We have fine voluntary control of breathing, tongue and lips, and the hypoglossal canal that carries the tongue's nerve is wider in Homo sapiens and Neanderthals than in earlier hominins. The gene FOXP2, which in humans differs from the chimpanzee version by two amino acids, is involved in the motor control of speech; people with a damaged copy struggle to sequence sounds and apply grammatical rules. None of this is a "language gene", but together it shows the body was reshaped around talking, as the gut was reshaped around cooking.

When it happened

Speech leaves no fossils, so the dating is indirect. The anatomy, including the hyoid bone and an ear tuned to speech frequencies, is in place in Neanderthals and early Homo sapiens by around 300,000 years ago, so some form of speech is likely that old. What can be dated is behaviour that seems to need language: engraved ochre and shell beads at Blombos Cave in South Africa by about 75,000 years ago, long-distance exchange of raw materials, burials with grave goods, and the flowering of art, music and specialized tools after roughly 50,000 years ago. The conservative reading is that modern language was present by 100,000 years ago and fully elaborated by 50,000.

Why it evolved

The anthropologist Robin Dunbar offered the most useful account. Primates hold groups together by grooming, which builds trust one pair at a time and takes hours a day, so group size is limited by available time. Dunbar found that across primates, neocortex size predicts group size, and the human brain predicts a group of about 150, too many to groom. Language, in his account, is grooming at scale: one speaker reaches several listeners, and what they exchange is gossip, which is information about who can be trusted. The next chapter takes up the 150.

Whatever the first driver, the pay-off was teaching. A chimpanzee mother does not teach her infant to crack nuts; the infant watches for years and mostly works it out. A human parent can say "not that rock, this one, and hold it like this". Instruction compresses a lifetime of trial and error into an afternoon.

The ratchet

The psychologist Michael Tomasello calls it the ratchet effect: an improvement, once made, is passed on faithfully enough that the next generation starts from it instead of rediscovering it, so improvements stack. Chimpanzee cultures exist, but they do not stack. Human culture does, and language is the pawl that stops the ratchet slipping back.

Look at the previous two chapters this way. The hand-axe stayed the same for a million years, which is what a technology looks like without a ratchet: each maker learned by watching, got about as good as the model, and could not transmit a refinement that was hard to see. Fire-keeping, with its sequences of fuel, airflow and tinder, is far easier to pass on in words. The acceleration after 50,000 years ago is what you would expect once good ideas stopped dying with their inventors.

What it forced in the other threads

Language reorganized the band. Plans could be made and roles assigned, so a hunt became a coordinated drive, which is force applied through information. Reputation became a currency: a known sharer could draw on others in lean times, the seed of credit and the subject of chapter 5. Kinship could be named across generations, which made alliances between bands possible, and with them the long-distance exchange of obsidian and shell in chapter 7. Stories at the fire became a group's memory of waterholes, routes and dangers: an oral database, maintained by retelling, long before any clay tablet.

Language also created something new to fight over. Myths, laws and gods are made of words, and a group that shares them can coordinate at a scale kinship cannot reach. That is the capability Sumerian temples and Egyptian kings will build on, and it is why this history can be told as a sequence of information technologies: speech, writing, the alphabet, print, each widening the circle of minds that can hold one idea at once.

The limits

Speech is cheap to produce and cheap to lose. It travels as far as a voice and lasts as long as a memory. Everything a band knew lived in its living members and had to be recited to survive them; one bad decade could erase a skill. Writing will solve exactly that problem, and it will take another ninety thousand years to arrive.

Keep

  • Language is the first information technology: a combinatorial system that can describe things absent, past, future and imaginary, which no animal call system can do.
  • The body was reshaped around speech, with a lowered larynx, fine breath control and a wider nerve canal to the tongue, which tells us speech is old, probably over 300,000 years.
  • Its decisive effect is the ratchet: taught knowledge accumulates across generations instead of resetting, which is why technology accelerated after 50,000 years ago.
  • Dunbar's account ties language to group size: talk is grooming at scale, and gossip is information about trust.
  • Speech dies with its speakers, and that weakness is the problem writing will eventually solve.

Names worth knowing

  • Robin Dunbar — British anthropologist who linked primate brain size to group size, proposed about 150 as the natural human group, and argued that language evolved as grooming at scale.
  • Michael Tomasello — Developmental psychologist who named the ratchet effect: humans transmit culture faithfully enough that improvements accumulate, while other apes' cultures do not.
