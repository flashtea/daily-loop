History of Civilization · Chapter 3 · Before civilization

Fire

Cooked food, the bigger brain, the longer day, and the first energy technology

5 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

With fire, people began to borrow energy from something other than their own bodies. A cooked root or a roasted joint gives up far more of itself than the raw thing, and a lineage that cooked could afford a smaller gut and a larger brain. Fire also lit the evening, gathered the band around one spot, and put the first heat into the hands of the toolmaker, from the hardened spear point to the kiln.

Thirty metres inside Wonderwerk Cave, in the Northern Cape of South Africa, where no lightning could reach and no grass fire could burn, there is a layer of ash and charred bone about a million years old. Someone carried fire in there, fed it and sat by it. Older traces exist, burnt bones at Swartkrans and at Koobi Fora from around 1.5 million years ago, but a wildfire leaves the same marks on a bone that a hearth does, and nobody can say whether those fires were kept or merely survived. Wonderwerk is the earliest place where the fire was plainly somebody's. By 400,000 years ago, at Qesem Cave in Israel and at sites in England and Germany, hearths are ordinary, used again and again, with the ash of many seasons stacked one on another.

Between those dates Homo erectus and its descendants went from tolerating fire to keeping it, and at some point from keeping it to making it. That was a slow business, and worth describing, because for most of the time that people have had fire they could not make it.

Keeping it and making it

A fire needs fuel, air and heat together, and a keeper's whole craft lies in managing the three. Starve it of air and it smokes and dies; give it too much and it eats its fuel and dies; let it cool below the point where wood gives off burnable gas and the flame goes out and will not come back. A band that had caught fire from a lightning-struck tree had to feed it, bank it under ash at night, carry it between camps as an ember in a bundle of dry fungus or dung, and keep rain off it. Losing the fire meant walking, perhaps for days, to beg a coal from another band. Whoever kept the fire held a responsible office.

Making fire came later and by two routes. One was friction: a hardwood spindle spun in a notch of softer wood, between the palms or with a bow, until the hot dust at the base glows and can be tipped into tinder and blown into flame. The other was percussion: a lump of pyrite struck against flint throws sparks that are fragments of hot metal, and with dry tinder and patience the sparks catch. Both methods demand skill and good materials, and on a wet day neither works, so that even people who could make fire preferred to keep it.

What cooking does

Cooking is a way of doing part of digestion outside the body. Heat breaks down starch and unwinds the proteins of meat, so that the gut can take far more from a cooked tuber or a roasted joint than from the raw thing, and in a fraction of the time. A chimpanzee spends about six hours a day chewing; a human spends about one. Fire also kills the parasites in meat and the poisons in many plants, which made a great many things edible that had been dangerous before.

The anthropologist Richard Wrangham argued in 2009 that cooking, more than meat, is what turned a clever ape into something like a human. Compared with other apes of the same size, humans have small teeth, weak jaws, a short gut and an enormous brain. Brain and gut are both costly tissue, and in 1995 Leslie Aiello and Peter Wheeler proposed that the one had paid for the other: a smaller digestive system freed the energy a larger brain demands. Cooking is what made the trade affordable. Brain size roughly doubled between early Homo erectus and modern humans, and the steepest part of the climb comes after hearths become common. Whether fire drove the change or merely permitted it is still argued over. The lineage that cooked is the one that ended up with the brain.

The evening

On the savanna the sun goes down at about six, and for every other primate the night is spent asleep in a tree, out of reach of leopards. Fire pushed the leopards back and lit a circle of ground, and the people inside the circle gained several waking hours a night in which nothing could be gathered or hunted. The hours went to mending, to toolmaking and to talk. Polly Wiessner, who spent years recording the conversations of the Ju/'hoansi in the Kalahari, found that daytime talk is mostly business, about food, plans and complaints, while talk by the fire is mostly stories, about people far away and long ago. The hearth is where a band's memory began to live in common rather than in separate heads.

It also fixed the band in place. Food had to be brought back to the fire to be cooked, which meant a home base, and a home base meant sharing, and sharing meant the first arguments about who got what.

Fire in the toolmaker's hands

Once fire was tame the toolmakers took it up. The wooden spears found at Schöningen in Germany, about 300,000 years old, have tips hardened in embers. By 160,000 years ago at Pinnacle Point on the South African coast, knappers were heating silcrete in the fire before working it, because heated stone flakes more cleanly. Birch bark cooked slowly without air yields a black tar that fixes a stone point to a shaft, and Neanderthals were making it. Each of these is a small step on a road that leads a long way: clay turns to pottery at about 800 degrees, copper runs out of its ore at about 1,100, and iron needs hotter still. A furnace is a hearth with its draught under control.

People also turned fire on the land. Hunters on every continent burned off old grass to drive game and to bring on the fresh growth that grazing animals come to, and over tens of thousands of years the Aboriginal peoples of Australia shaped the vegetation of a continent this way. To change what grows across a whole country at will is a power no other animal has ever held. The plough and the irrigation canal are its descendants.
