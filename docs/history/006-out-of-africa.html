Out of Africa

Migration, the peopling of the continents, and the Neanderthal encounter

13 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

Between about 70,000 and 15,000 years ago a tropical ape that had spent its whole existence in Africa reached every habitable continent, crossed open sea, lived through an ice age and replaced every other kind of human. The capability behind it was a package: fire, sewn clothing, projectile weapons, boats, and knowledge carried by language through wide social networks. The result is a species that adapts by culture rather than by body.

Every chapter so far has happened in Africa. By 100,000 years ago Homo sapiens had stone tools, fire, language and the band economy of the last chapter, and had them only there. Eurasia belonged to other humans: Neanderthals in the west and Denisovans in the east, both adapted to cold over hundreds of thousands of years. Within a span that is short on this series' clock, sapiens reached every continent except Antarctica and ended up as the only human species left.

Leaving was easy; staying was hard

People had been leaving Africa long before the migration that mattered. A jawbone from Misliya cave in Israel is about 180,000 years old and looks fully modern, and the skeletons from Skhul and Qafzeh nearby are around 100,000 years old. So the route north through the Sinai was known and used whenever the climate made it passable. What those early groups did not manage was to stay. As far as the genetics can tell, none of them left descendants among people living today; the corridor closed with the climate, and when it closed the Neanderthals held it.

The wave that stuck left roughly 70,000 to 60,000 years ago, and it was small. Everyone alive today outside Africa descends from a founding population in the low thousands, which is why non-Africans carry only a fraction of the genetic variety found within Africa. From Arabia the line of settlement ran east along the Indian Ocean coasts. People were in Australia by about 65,000 years ago, which required crossing some ninety kilometres of open water that no land bridge ever closed, the oldest sea voyage that can be proved. They were in Siberia and Europe by about 45,000 years ago, in the Americas by about 16,000 years ago over the dry Bering land bridge, and at the southern tip of South America a few thousand years after that.

How a tropical ape lived through an ice age

The Neanderthals had solved cold the slow way. Over 300,000 years their bodies had become short-limbed and barrel-chested to hold heat. Sapiens solved it in a few generations without changing shape, by wrapping technology around the same body. Fire turned a cave into a warm room and a Siberian night into a working evening, and sewn clothing came next. Eyed needles of bone appear by about 40,000 years ago, and the genetics of body lice, which live only in clothing, suggest fitted garments are considerably older. A hide tunic sewn shut with sinew keeps a sleeper alive at temperatures that kill someone wrapped in a loose skin.

Then there was the question of food. Killing a large animal with a hand-held spear is close, dangerous work that favours a heavy frame, and Neanderthal skeletons carry the injuries of it. Sapiens hunters increasingly killed from a distance, with throwing spears, spear-throwers and eventually the bow, so a lighter body cost them nothing and a hunt risked less. Boats, which Australia proves existed though none survive, opened coasts and islands.

None of this would have worked without the information layer. A Neanderthal band learned the land it lived on. A sapiens band could inherit, in words, knowledge of lands it had never seen: which roots are safe here, how the river behaves in spring, where the herds cross. The networks of about 150 people from the last chapter began to leave physical traces, shell beads and ochre worn as markers of identity, and good stone moving hundreds of kilometres from its source. A band that hit a bad winter could fall back on relatives three valleys over, and Neanderthals, who lived at low density in small scattered groups, apparently could not.

The encounter

For at least 5,000 years in Europe, and longer in the Near East, the two kinds of human shared a continent. Neanderthals had brains as large as modern ones, kept fire, hafted their tools with birch tar, buried some of their dead and, at a few sites in Croatia and Italy, wore eagle talons as ornaments. What they lacked was numbers. The best estimates put the whole Neanderthal population of Eurasia at a few tens of thousands at any one time, with the low genetic diversity of a species that had been small for a long while. When sapiens arrived at higher densities and with wider networks, the Neanderthals did not need to be fought to disappear. Losing a little every generation in the competition for the same animals and the same caves was enough, and by about 40,000 years ago they were gone as a separate population.

In 2010 Svante Pääbo's team at the Max Planck Institute in Leipzig published the first Neanderthal genome and found that people outside Africa carry about 1 to 2 percent Neanderthal DNA; the two had interbred, probably in the Near East, before the main dispersal fanned out. The same year a finger bone from Denisova cave in the Altai mountains of Siberia yielded the genome of a human nobody had named, and Denisovan DNA turned out to make up several percent of the genomes of Papuans and Aboriginal Australians. The variant of the EPAS1 gene that lets Tibetans live at high altitude came from Denisovans. The encounter ended in absorption as much as in replacement.

What it forced

Wherever people arrived, the largest animals soon dwindled: the giant marsupials of Australia, the mammoths and ground sloths of the Americas. Climate played a part, and the timing follows the human footprints too closely to be coincidence. The loss of easy big game pushed hunters toward smaller prey, fish, plants and eventually, in some places, farming. The founding bottleneck kept the whole species genetically closer than two neighbouring groups of chimpanzees, whatever the surface differences that drifted in afterwards. From here on, the differences between peoples are differences of tools, institutions and recorded knowledge, and the series' working rule, that technology compounds while human nature does not, rests on that fact.

By the time the ice began to retreat, one human species held the planet, spread thin across every climate, carrying its adaptations in its hands and heads rather than in its bones. The next chapter looks inside those hands: the bow, the eyed needle and the boat, and the first trade in obsidian and shells, which shows the networks beginning to carry goods as well as people.
