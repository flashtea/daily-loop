History of Civilization · Chapter 6 · Before civilization

Out of Africa

Migration, the peopling of the continents, and the Neanderthal encounter

13 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

A hundred thousand years ago modern humans lived only in Africa, and the rest of the Old World belonged to other kinds of people who had been adapting to its cold for hundreds of thousands of years. Within fifty thousand years the Africans had reached every habitable continent, crossed open sea and lived through an ice age, and they were the only humans left. They did it without changing their bodies, by carrying their adaptations in their hands and in their heads.

A hundred thousand years ago the world had several kinds of human in it. Homo sapiens lived in Africa, and only there, with stone tools, fire, language and the small wandering bands of the forager. Europe and western Asia belonged to the Neanderthals, heavy, strong, big-brained people who had been living with the cold for three hundred thousand years. Further east, across Siberia and down into Southeast Asia, lived the Denisovans, known today from a finger bone, a few teeth and the genes they left behind. Within a span that is short on the scale of this story, one of these peoples reached every continent except Antarctica and the others vanished.

Leaving was easy

People had been walking out of Africa for a long time before the departure that mattered. A jawbone from Misliya Cave on Mount Carmel is about 180,000 years old and fully modern, and the skeletons from the nearby caves of Skhul and Qafzeh, buried with grave goods, are about 100,000 years old. What those early emigrants could not do was stay. None of them has descendants alive today; the corridor closed with the climate, and when it closed the Neanderthals held the far end.

The wave that lasted left between about 70,000 and 60,000 years ago, and it was small. Everyone now alive outside Africa descends from a founding population of a few thousand, which is why two villages in Africa can differ from each other more than a Swede differs from a Papuan. From Arabia the settlers followed the coasts of the Indian Ocean east. They were in Australia by about 65,000 years ago, which meant crossing at least ninety kilometres of open sea that no land bridge ever closed, the first ocean voyage that can be proved. They were in Siberia and Europe by about 45,000 years ago, walked into the Americas over the dry floor of the Bering Strait around 16,000 years ago, and reached the tip of South America a few thousand years after that.

How a tropical ape survived the ice

The Neanderthals had solved the cold the slow way, over three hundred thousand years, by becoming short-limbed and barrel-chested so as to hold their heat. Sapiens solved it in a few generations without changing shape, by wrapping technology round the body it already had. Fire made a cave into a warm room and a Siberian winter night into a working evening. Clothing came next: bone needles with eyes appear by about 40,000 years ago, and the genes of the body louse, which lives only in clothes, suggest that fitted garments are a good deal older.

Then there was the matter of food. Killing a horse or a bison with a hand-held spear is close, dangerous work that favours a heavy frame, and Neanderthal skeletons carry the fractures of it, in the pattern rodeo riders show today. Sapiens hunters increasingly killed from a distance, with throwing spears, spear-throwers and in time the bow, so that a light body cost them nothing and a hunt risked less.

None of it would have worked without the knowledge that travelled with them. A Neanderthal band knew the land it lived on. A sapiens band could inherit, in words, the knowledge of lands it had never seen: which roots were safe, how the river ran in spring, where the herds crossed. The networks of a few hundred people began to leave physical traces, shell beads and red ochre worn as badges of who one was, and fine stone carried hundreds of kilometres from its source. A band that struck a bad winter could fall back on kin three valleys away. The Neanderthals, who lived thin on the ground in small, scattered groups, seem to have had no such safety net.

The meeting

For at least five thousand years in Europe, and longer in the Near East, the two kinds of people shared a continent. The Neanderthals were no brutes. Their brains were as large as a modern human's, they kept fire, hafted their tools with birch tar, buried some of their dead, and at a few sites in Croatia and Italy wore eagle talons as ornaments. What they lacked was numbers. The best estimates put the whole Neanderthal population of Eurasia at a few tens of thousands at any one time, with the thin genetic variety of a people who had been few for a long while. When sapiens arrived, thicker on the ground and better connected, nobody needed to fight. Losing a little every generation in the contest for the same animals and the same caves was enough, and by about 40,000 years ago the Neanderthals were gone as a separate people.

Something of them survives. In 2010 Svante Pääbo's team at the Max Planck Institute in Leipzig published the first Neanderthal genome and found that everyone outside Africa carries one to two percent of it; the two peoples had interbred before the main dispersal fanned out. The same year a finger bone from Denisova Cave in the Altai yielded the genome of a human nobody had known existed, and Denisovan ancestry turned out to make up several percent of the genomes of Papuans and Aboriginal Australians. The meeting ended in absorption as much as in replacement.

What followed

Wherever people arrived, the largest animals soon dwindled: the giant kangaroos and the rhinoceros-sized wombats of Australia, the mammoths, ground sloths and horses of the Americas. Climate played a part, and the timing follows the human footprints too closely for climate to be the whole story. The loss of easy big game pushed hunters toward smaller prey, fish, shellfish and plants, and in a few places, eventually, toward farming. The founding bottleneck left the whole species more alike than two neighbouring groups of chimpanzees. From here on the differences between peoples are differences of tools, of institutions and of what has been written down.

By the time the ice began to retreat, one species of human held the planet, spread thin across every climate, carrying its adaptations in its hands and heads rather than in its bones.
