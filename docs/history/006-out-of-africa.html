Out of Africa

Migration, the peopling of the continents, and the Neanderthal encounter

13 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

Between about 70,000 and 15,000 years ago a tropical ape that had spent its whole existence in Africa reached every habitable continent, crossed open sea, lived through an ice age and replaced every other kind of human. The capability behind it was not a single tool but a package: fire, sewn clothing, projectile weapons, boats and, above all, knowledge carried by language through wide social networks. The result is a species that adapts by culture rather than by body, which is why everything that follows in this series is history rather than biology.

Every chapter so far has happened in Africa. By 100,000 years ago our species had stone tools, fire, language and the band economy of the last chapter, and it had them only there. Eurasia belonged to other humans: Neanderthals in the west, Denisovans in the east, both adapted to cold over hundreds of thousands of years. Within a span that is short on this series' clock, Homo sapiens walked, paddled and hunted its way to every continent except Antarctica and ended up alone. This chapter is about the capability that made that possible, because the rest of the story runs on it.

Leaving was easy; staying was hard

Africa was not a sealed box. Modern-looking jaws and skulls turn up in the Levant at Misliya cave about 180,000 years ago and at Skhul and Qafzeh around 100,000 years ago. Those people got out. As far as living genomes can tell, they left no descendants: the Levant was a corridor that opened and closed with the climate, and when it closed, the Neanderthals held it. Walking across the Sinai was never the problem. Living, generation after generation, in country a tropical primate was not built for was the problem.

The wave that stuck left roughly 70,000 to 60,000 years ago, and it was small. Everyone alive today outside Africa descends from a founding population in the low thousands, which is why non-Africans carry only a fraction of the genetic variety found within Africa. From Arabia the line of settlement ran east along the Indian Ocean coasts. People were in Australia by about 65,000 years ago, across some ninety kilometres of open water that no land bridge ever closed, the oldest sea voyage we can prove. They were in Siberia and Europe by about 45,000 years ago, in the Americas by about 16,000 years ago over the dry Bering land bridge, and at the tip of South America a few thousand years later. Averaged out, the frontier moved a few kilometres a year: a band deciding each season to camp one valley further on.

How a tropical ape lived in the ice

The Neanderthals had solved cold the slow way. Over 300,000 years their bodies became short-limbed and barrel-chested to hold heat. Sapiens solved it in a few generations without changing shape, by wrapping technology around the same body. Fire was the base layer: it turned a cave into a warm room and a Siberian night into a working evening. Sewn clothing came next. Eyed bone needles appear by about 40,000 years ago, and the genetics of body lice, which live only in clothing, suggest fitted garments are older still. A hide tunic sewn shut with sinew keeps a sleeper alive at temperatures that kill someone wrapped in a loose skin.

Then came the means to eat. Killing a large animal with a hand-held spear is close, dangerous work that favours a heavy frame; Neanderthal skeletons carry the injuries of it. Sapiens hunters increasingly killed from distance, with throwing spears, spear-throwers and eventually the bow, so a lighter body cost nothing and a hunt risked less. Boats, which nobody preserves but Australia proves, opened coasts and islands to people who could not reach them on foot.

None of this worked without the information layer. A Neanderthal band learned the land it lived on; a sapiens band could inherit, in words, knowledge of lands it had never seen: which roots are safe here, how the river behaves in spring, where the herds cross. The networks of about 150 people from the last chapter began to leave physical traces: shell beads and ochre worn as identity markers, and good stone moving hundreds of kilometres from its source. A band that hit a bad winter could fall back on relatives three valleys over. Neanderthals, living at low density in small scattered groups, apparently could not, and that difference compounds.

The encounter

For at least 5,000 years in Europe, and longer in the Near East, the two kinds of human shared a continent. Neanderthals were not the brutes of the old cartoons: brains as large as ours, fire, tools hafted with birch tar, some burials, at a few sites eagle talons worn as ornaments. What they lacked was numbers. The best estimates put all the Neanderthals in Eurasia at a few tens of thousands at any one time, with the low genetic diversity of a species long kept small. When sapiens arrived at higher densities with wider networks, the Neanderthals did not need to be fought to disappear. They needed only to lose a little, every generation, in the competition for the same animals and the same caves. By about 40,000 years ago they were gone.

Gone is not quite the word. In 2010 Svante Pääbo's team published the first Neanderthal genome and found that people outside Africa carry about 1 to 2 percent Neanderthal DNA; the two had interbred, probably in the Near East, before the main dispersal fanned out. The same year a finger bone from Denisova cave in Siberia yielded the genome of a human nobody had named, and Denisovan DNA turned out to make up several percent of the genomes of Papuans and Aboriginal Australians. The variant that lets Tibetans live at altitude came from them. The encounter ended in absorption as much as replacement.

What it forced

Wherever people arrived, the largest animals soon dwindled: the giant marsupials of Australia, the mammoths and ground sloths of the Americas. Climate played a part, but the timing follows the footprints too closely for coincidence, and the loss of easy big game pushed hunters toward smaller prey, fish, plants and eventually, in some places, farming. Isolated for tens of thousands of years, regional populations drifted into the surface differences we notice today, yet the founding bottleneck kept the whole species genetically closer than two neighbouring groups of chimpanzees. From here on, the differences between peoples are differences of tools, institutions and recorded knowledge, not of kind. That is the warrant for this series' rule: technology compounds, human nature does not.

By the time the ice began to retreat, one human species held the planet, spread thin across every climate, carrying its adaptations in its hands and heads rather than its bones. The next chapter looks inside those hands: the bow, the eyed needle and the boat, and the first trade in obsidian and shells that shows the networks beginning to carry goods as well as people.

Keep

  • Several early sapiens groups left Africa and vanished; the wave that stuck, around 70,000 to 60,000 years ago, was a founding population of a few thousand from whom all non-Africans descend.
  • Neanderthals adapted to cold with their bodies over 300,000 years; sapiens did it in generations with fire, sewn clothing, projectile weapons and boats wrapped around an unchanged body.
  • The decisive edge was information: language let a band inherit knowledge of places it had never seen, and wide networks marked by beads and traded shells gave it somewhere to fall back on.
  • Neanderthals disappeared through numbers, not battle, and partly by absorption; non-Africans carry 1 to 2 percent Neanderthal DNA, and Denisovan genes still help Tibetans breathe at altitude.
  • After the dispersal, human differences are differences of tools and institutions, not biology, which is why the rest of this series is history.

Names worth knowing

  • Svante Pääbo — Swedish geneticist whose team sequenced the Neanderthal genome in 2010 and identified the Denisovans from a single finger bone, proving that modern humans interbred with both; Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, 2022.
