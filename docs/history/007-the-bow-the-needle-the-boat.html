History of Civilization · Chapter 7 · Before civilization

The bow, the needle, the boat

The Upper Paleolithic toolkit and the first long-distance trade

15 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

In the last fifty thousand years of the Ice Age the toolkit changed more than it had in the previous two million. A hunter at the end of it carried a bow, wore sewn clothes, and might have been born on an island his grandparents reached by boat, and the shells on his necklace had come from a sea he had never seen. With those shells, passed from hand to hand across hundreds of kilometres, something that looks very like money enters the story.

At Sungir, outside the town of Vladimir in Russia, the archaeologist Otto Bader opened in 1964 the grave of a man who had been buried about 34,000 years ago. He had been laid out in clothes sewn all over with beads of mammoth ivory, nearly three thousand of them, each drilled and polished and each, by experiment, about an hour's work, so that the suit represents something like a year of one person's working days. Whoever he was, people had thought him worth a great deal, and they had what it took to show it: needles to sew the beads on, drills to pierce them, and a settled enough way of life, on the Russian plain at the height of the ice, to spend a year on a dead man's coat.

Two million years of hand-axes had produced nothing like him. In the last fifty thousand years of the Ice Age the tools changed every few thousand years instead of every few hundred thousand, and by its end a hunter in the south of France carried a spear-thrower and a bow, bone needles and fish hooks, a stone lamp, and a necklace of shells from a coast he might never have seen. Once a good design could be put into words and carried along a network of kin, it no longer died with its maker.

The bow

A thrown spear leaves the hand at perhaps twenty metres a second, because an arm can only push for the length of its own swing. A spear-thrower lengthens the swing and adds something to the speed. The bow does something new. Drawing it bends two limbs of wood, and the work of drawing is stored in the bent fibres; releasing the string returns all of it to a thirty-gram arrow in about a hundredth of a second. The archer's muscles work slowly and the arrow leaves fast. It is the first machine built to store energy and let it go on a trigger, and it fires from a crouch, in silence, and can be drawn again in three seconds.

When it was invented is argued. Marlize Lombard and Laurel Phillipson claimed in 2010 that small quartz points from Sibudu Cave in South Africa, about 64,000 years old, carry the impact fractures and traces of glue of arrowheads, and the claim is disputed, because a point proves only a point. The oldest arrows that survive are pine shafts from Stellmoor, near Hamburg, about 12,000 years old. The oldest bows are two staves of elm from a bog at Holmegaard in Denmark, around 9,000 years old, one of them 154 centimetres long and shaped so that the limbs thin toward the tips, which is still how a wooden bow is made.

The needle

A loose hide thrown round the shoulders leaks warmth at every gap. A garment sewn to the shape of the body, worn in layers, holds a shell of still air against the skin, and making one needs an awl to punch the hole, a needle to pull sinew thread through it, and an eye small enough that the thread stays in. Needles of bone with eyes turn up by about 40,000 years ago in Europe and Siberia. On the Yana river, inside the Arctic Circle, people were living through the winters by 32,000 years ago, at a latitude where a Neanderthal in a loose cloak seems never to have managed to stay.

The boat

No boat of the period survives; wood rots, and the coasts where boats were used are now under a hundred metres of sea. The evidence is where people turned up. Australia was reached by about 65,000 years ago across stretches of open water that were never dry, the widest some ninety kilometres, and the islands of Japan and the Bismarck Archipelago off New Guinea by 35,000. A crossing like that calls for something that will carry a founding group with food and water for a day or two and can be steered, which means a raft of logs with paddles, and more likely a hull of bark or hide. The crossings were almost certainly deliberate, because the islands beyond were settled on a scale that castaways do not produce. A boat also changes what a coast is worth. Shellfish and seabirds are there every day, and a band that could work the shore from the water could stay through seasons that would once have forced a move.

The things that travelled

Obsidian is volcanic glass. It breaks to an edge a few molecules thick, and every volcano's glass carries its own trace of rare elements, so that a flake can be matched to the flow it came from, as Colin Renfrew and two colleagues showed with a spectrometer in the 1960s. At Franchthi Cave in the Peloponnese, obsidian from the island of Melos, 120 kilometres away across open water, appears in layers 13,000 years old.

Shells settle the question, because a sea shell at an inland site cannot have been gathered. At Blombos the drilled snail shells are 75,000 years old. At Kostenki on the Don, shell beads had come 500 kilometres from the Black Sea. They are useless as tools. They were pierced, strung, worn and buried with the dead, and they had passed from hand to hand down chains of people none of whom had seen the sea.

Nick Szabo has argued that these beads were the first money: scarce, lasting, hard to fake, easy to carry, and valued by everyone along the network, so that a debt between bands could be settled in them long before anyone could count. Most archaeologists stop short of that and call them tokens of identity and alliance. The two readings may be looking at the same objects from opposite ends, since a string of shells given at a marriage and a string given for a winter's shelter are both claims on future help, and nothing in the ground tells them apart. What the shells do prove is the network: chains of exchange hundreds of kilometres long, kept up by people who never saw where the things came from.
