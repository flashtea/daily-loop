History of Civilization · Chapter 1 · Before civilization

Why study the whole arc

Five threads from the first stone tool to the present

3 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

History read as a parade of kings is mostly noise. Read as the story of how people learned to capture energy, make tools, keep and move knowledge, trade with strangers and fight, it becomes one long process with a few rules that still hold. This opening chapter lays out those five threads and the way the chapters that follow will use them.

Will Durant published the first volume of The Story of Civilization in 1935 and the eleventh in 1975, and the eleventh ended with Napoleon. His wife Ariel, who had been his pupil and became his co-author, died in October 1981, and he died thirteen days later. Few people will read all ten thousand pages, and the Durants knew it; in 1968, with ten volumes behind them, they published a short book called The Lessons of History that tried to say what the rest had taught them. Their answer was that the kings and the battles were the least of it. What had stayed with them, after a working lifetime among the sources, was a smaller number of changes in what human beings could do, and the ways people arranged their lives once they could do it.

The chapters that follow take that answer as their plan. They go in order from the first chipped stone to the present and spend the most time where the changes came fastest, and five threads run through all of them.

Energy

Every society lives inside the energy it can lay hands on. For almost the whole of the human past that energy was food, turned into work by muscle, and a grown man labouring all day delivers about as much power as a dim light bulb. Fire added heat to the muscle. The ox and the horse added muscle that was not one's own, and the water wheel and the windmill borrowed from the river and the sky. Then coal, oil and electricity took the ceiling off, and the world of the past two centuries is what happened when it came off. A people that is expanding has usually found a new source of energy, and a people that is stalling has usually run an old one down.

Tools

A tool is knowledge made solid. The hand-axe was made the same way for a million years. Bronze took a few thousand years to work out, iron a few hundred, the steam engine a few decades, the transistor a few years. The shortening of that interval, from a million years to a season, is the plainest measure there is of how fast people learn, and it is the one movement in this story that has never gone into reverse for long.

Information

Speech let what one person knew outlive that person. Writing let it outlive the village. The alphabet, paper, the printing press, the telegraph and the network each made it cheaper to copy a thought and send it somewhere, and each time the price fell the order of things shook, with new religions and new sciences and more than once a new politics. Whoever holds the cheapest channel holds a great deal, and never holds it for long, because a cheaper channel is always on its way.

Money

Money is how strangers trade. It carries value across time and across distance between people who have no reason to trust one another, and it has been made of barley, of silver weighed on a scale, of stamped coins, of paper, of entries in a bank's ledger and of numbers in a shared computer file. Every form has met the same temptation, which is that whoever issues money can issue more of it. A good part of the history of money is the history of that temptation and of the arrangements people made to resist it or to profit by it.

Force

Who can hurt whom, and at what price, decides who rules. The chariot, the composite bow, iron, the stirrup, the cannon, the machine gun and the bomb each moved power from one group to another, nearly always toward whoever could afford the new thing and organise around it. Most histories put force in front. Here it comes last of the five, because on inspection it is usually downstream of the other four: the army that wins is generally the one whose society found the energy, the tools, the knowledge and the money first.

Two rules

Two rules hold across the whole story. The first is that technology compounds and human nature does not. A Sumerian scribe would recognise nothing of the machinery of the modern world and almost everything of its office politics; the envy, the status-seeking, the loyalty to one's own and the short-sightedness pressed into his clay tablets four thousand years ago are the stuff of any newspaper.

The second is that a change in one thread forces changes in the others, and the forcing is where most of the story lives. Farming produced surplus; surplus had to be counted; counting became writing; writing made taxes possible; taxes paid for armies, which took more land, which grew more grain. The printing press made Luther possible. The telegraph made a continental railway workable, the railway made a national market, and the national market wanted a national currency. Watching these chains run is most of what it means to understand history, and it is a good deal more use than remembering which king followed which.

What follows tries to do a few things consistently. People appear when they turned the course of events and are left out when they were merely present. Wherever possible a chapter explains how a thing worked, a plough or a bill of exchange or a cannon, well enough that the reader could explain it to someone else, because a thing understood that way stays understood. And every chapter asks the same question of its era: which thread moved, and what did that movement make everyone else do.
