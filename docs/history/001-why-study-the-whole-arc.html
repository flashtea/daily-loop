History of Civilization · Chapter 1 · Before civilization

Why study the whole arc

Five threads from the first stone tool to the first software agent

3 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

History read as a sequence of kings is mostly noise. Read as the record of how humans captured energy, built tools, stored information, organised money and projected force, it becomes one compounding process with a few rules that still hold. This chapter sets out those five threads and the lens the series will use.

Will Durant spent forty years writing eleven volumes on the story of civilization and still stopped at Napoleon. Nobody is going to read that on a phone over coffee. His instinct, though, was sound: the parts of history worth carrying around are the handful of changes that altered what a human being could do, and the ways people organised themselves once they could do it. The dates and the kings are the smallest part.

This series follows five threads. Each was chosen because it is a capability, something that compounds when it improves, and because almost every event that mattered touched at least one of them.

Energy

Everything a society does is bounded by the energy it can capture. For most of human existence that meant food, converted by muscle, which gives an adult roughly 100 watts of continuous output. Fire added heat, and domesticated animals multiplied muscle, since an ox delivers several times what a man can. Water and wind turned mills. Coal, oil and electricity then removed the ceiling, and the modern world is the result. A civilization that is expanding has usually found a new source; one that is stalling has usually exhausted an old one.

Tools

A tool is knowledge stored in physical form. The hand-axe stayed essentially unchanged for a million years. Bronze metallurgy took a few thousand years to mature, iron a few hundred. The steam engine was improved within decades of its invention, the transistor within years, and the software on the reader's phone was updated last night. The interval between improvements is the clearest single measure of how fast a society learns.

Information

Speech let knowledge outlive the person who held it. Writing let it outlive the village. The alphabet, paper, printing, the telegraph and the network each cut the cost of copying and moving a thought by a large factor, and each cut was followed by upheaval: new religions, new sciences, new politics. Whoever controls the cheapest channel of information holds real power, and the control never lasts, because the next channel is always cheaper.

Money

Money is the information technology of trade, a way to store value across time and move it across space between parties who do not trust each other. Barley, silver by weight, stamped coins, paper notes, bank ledgers and digital tokens are attempts to solve that one problem, and each has been tested by the same temptation: whoever issues money can issue more of it. The history of money is largely the history of that temptation and of the institutions built to resist it or to exploit it. The Saylor series follows this thread most closely, and this series borrows its lens from it.

Force

Who can hurt whom, and at what cost, determines who rules. The chariot, the composite bow, iron weapons, the stirrup, the cannon, the machine gun and the atomic bomb each redistributed power, usually away from one group and toward whoever could afford and organise the new thing. Force is the thread most histories put in front. Here it is one of five, because it turns out to be downstream of the other four more often than upstream.

Two rules that hold throughout

Technology compounds and human nature does not. The tools of 2026 would be unrecognisable to a Sumerian scribe. The envy, status-seeking, tribal loyalty and short-sightedness recorded on his clay tablets would be familiar to him in any office today. A series on mental models runs alongside this one for that reason: the models are about the constant, the history is about the variable.

Every advance in one thread puts pressure on the others. Agriculture produced surplus, surplus required record-keeping, record-keeping produced writing, writing enabled taxation, and taxation funded armies. The printing press made Luther possible. The telegraph made continental railways manageable, railways made national markets, and national markets made national money. Watching these cascades is most of what understanding history consists of.

What the series will do

It moves roughly chronologically from the first stone tools to the present, in about two hundred chapters, spending more time where the threads changed fastest. It names people when the person was the hinge and skips them when they were merely present. It prefers explaining how a thing worked, a plough, a bill of exchange, a bombard, to listing who used it. And it asks the same question of every era: which of the five threads moved, and what did that movement force everyone else to do.
