History of Civilization · Chapter 5 · Before civilization

The hunter-gatherer economy

Band size, Dunbar's number, egalitarianism, mobility, and why wealth could not accumulate

11 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

For more than nine-tenths of the time there have been people, every one of them lived in a small wandering band that owned almost nothing, shared nearly everything and tolerated no boss. That was an economy fitted to wild food, which spoils and moves and cannot be piled up. Understanding why nobody could get rich in that world explains why everything changed the moment somebody could.

In 1963 a young anthropologist named Richard Lee went to live at Dobe, a cluster of waterholes on the edge of the Kalahari where Botswana meets Namibia, among the Ju/'hoansi, the !Kung of the older ethnographies. He wanted to know how foragers actually spent their days, and he kept a diary of what everyone in one camp did, hour by hour, through four weeks of a drought year in which the Bantu-speaking farmers nearby were on famine relief. Adults spent, on average, something over two days a week getting food, and the food came to around two thousand calories a day with plenty of protein. The rest of the time they visited, talked, rested and danced.

Marshall Sahlins made that finding famous in an essay called "The Original Affluent Society". Foragers were affluent, he argued, in the only way open to them: they wanted little, and the wanting was kept low by the fact that nothing could be kept. Later work took some of the shine off the numbers. Lee's hours left out cooking, firewood and water, and perhaps four children in ten did not live to grow up. The broad picture survived. A healthy forager band ate reasonably well for moderate work, and the first farmers, when they came, almost certainly worked harder and ate worse than their grandparents.

Why nothing could be kept

A band's food came from wild plants and animals spread thin across a landscape, and any one spot gave out fast. The nuts were picked, the roots within a morning's walk were dug, the game learned where the camp was. So the band moved, every few weeks, following the season from one patch to the next, and that fact ruled everything else. A family owned what it could carry, and when ethnographers weighed the whole kit of a Ju/'hoan household it came to a few kilograms: a digging stick, a bow and a quiver of poisoned arrows, a fire drill, water carried in ostrich eggshells, and the kaross, a leather cloak that doubled as the bag everything else went in. A heavier life was a slower and hungrier one.

Food could not be stored either. Meat rots in days in a warm climate, most gathered plants within a week, and anything cached and left behind belonged to whoever found it. The only durable place to put a surplus was into other people. A hunter who gave away meat today held a claim on everyone else's meat on the day his own hunt failed, and anthropologists who work with such bands call the arrangement an immediate-return economy: energy comes in and goes out within days, and the debts between people are the only savings.

The size of the world

A band was usually twenty to fifty people, a few related families, and bands belonged to larger networks of a few hundred that met in the good season, married across, and spoke one language. Joseph Birdsell, going through Australian census and mission records in the 1950s, found these sizes turning up so regularly that he treated them as part of the design of the way of life. The upper figure is Dunbar's. Around a hundred and fifty, a person can still keep track of who owes what to whom, and reputation does the work of law; above it cheating starts to pay, and the group splits. Any society that wants to be bigger than that and still hold together needs something to stand in for personal acquaintance: shared gods, written accounts, money, or a man with the force to compel. Each of those comes later in this story, and each is in part an answer to the problem of the hundred and fifty-first person.

Keeping everyone level

Forager bands were equal by constant effort, and the effort is visible in the record. Christopher Boehm, who went through the ethnographies of dozens of such societies, called the mechanism a reverse dominance hierarchy: the group as a whole sits on anyone who tries to rise above it, with ridicule first, then gossip, then refusal to share, then ostracism, and for the incorrigible, exile or death.

Lee recorded the classic case. A Ju/'hoan hunter who brought in a big animal was expected to speak of it with contempt, and his companions would examine the carcass and pronounce it a bag of bones, barely worth the carrying. When Lee asked why, an elder explained, in words Lee set down at the time, that a young man who kills much meat comes to think of himself as a chief and the rest as his servants, that this cannot be accepted, and that they speak of his meat as worthless to cool his heart and make him gentle. The hunter got no special share. In many bands the owner of the arrow, who was often not the man who shot it, was the nominal owner of the kill and did the dividing.

There was arithmetic under the ethic. In a world where nothing lasts, a good day cannot be turned into lasting power, and the only road to dominance is personal: being stronger, louder, more frightening. The band's weapons closed that road. A poisoned arrow lets a small man kill a large one from thirty paces, and a bully who had to sleep sometime was at the mercy of everyone he had bullied.

What could not happen

Without food that keeps there is no surplus, and the absences follow one from another. Nothing can be taxed or stolen in bulk, so nobody can be fed who does not forage, so there are no full-time priests, soldiers, scribes or kings. Nothing durable needs counting, so there is little use for numbers and none for writing. Nobody has a reason to stay, so nobody builds anything larger than a windbreak. Ninety thousand years passed in this way after tools, fire and language were all in hand, and they passed without a city or a coin, because a society whose wealth cannot be piled up gives nobody a place to stand above the rest.

All of it waited on one thing, a food that could be kept. When the ice retreated and a storable grain came within reach of people in the hills of the Near East, every constraint in this chapter came off at once.
