The hunter-gatherer economy

Band size, Dunbar's number, egalitarianism, mobility, and why wealth could not accumulate

11 October 2026 · that day's edition · all history of civilization

For more than 95 percent of the species' existence, every human lived in a small mobile band that owned almost nothing, shared almost everything and tolerated no boss. This was an economy tuned to its energy source, wild food that spoils, moves and cannot be stored, rather than a failure to invent property or hierarchy. Understanding why wealth could not accumulate in that world explains why everything changed the moment it could.

Tools, fire and language were in place by 100,000 years ago, and for the next ninety thousand years almost nothing that would now be called an economy appeared: no stores of wealth, no rulers, no cities, no money. The reason was the way energy was captured. Wild food cannot be stockpiled, and the social rules of the band were built around that fact and kept it so.

The energy budget of a band

A hunter-gatherer's energy came from wild plants and animals spread thinly across a landscape. Any one spot gave up its food quickly: the nuts were picked, the game learned to avoid the camp, the roots within a morning's walk were dug. So the band moved, typically every few weeks, following the season from one patch to the next.

Mobility set a hard ceiling on possessions. Everything a family owned had to be carried, usually by the women, while the men carried weapons and infants. Ethnographers who weighed the kit of twentieth-century foragers such as the Ju/'hoansi of the Kalahari found that a whole household's property came to a few kilograms: a digging stick, a bow and quiver, a fire drill, ostrich-egg water containers, and a kaross, the leather cloak that doubled as the bag to carry the rest in. A heavier life was a slower and hungrier one.

Food could not be stored either. Meat rots within days in a warm climate, most gathered plants within a week, and a cache left behind was a cache for someone else. The only durable store of surplus was other people. A hunter who gave away meat today held a claim on others' meat when his own hunt failed. Anthropologists call this an immediate-return system: energy comes in and goes out within days, and the social debts that result are the real savings account.

Band size and the Dunbar ceiling

The typical band was 20 to 50 people, a handful of related families, and bands clustered into networks of a few hundred that met seasonally, intermarried and shared a language. Joseph Birdsell, working on Australian census and mission records in the 1950s, found these sizes recurring so regularly that he treated them as a structural feature of the way of life.

The upper figure appeared in the chapter on language. Robin Dunbar's work on primate brains predicts that a human can keep track of about 150 relationships closely enough to know who owes whom, and the seasonal aggregations of foragers sit around that size. Below it, reputation does the work of law. Everyone knows who shares and who hoards, and a stingy hunter finds that nobody happens to be around when he needs help. Above it, cheating becomes possible, and the group splits.

The ceiling matters for everything that comes later. A society that wants to be larger than 150 people and still cooperate needs some technology to substitute for personal acquaintance: shared gods, written records, money, or a ruler with the force to compel. Each of those is a later chapter in this series.

Enforced equality

Hunter-gatherer bands were egalitarian by constant effort, and the effort is visible in the ethnography. Christopher Boehm, who surveyed dozens of forager societies, called the mechanism a reverse dominance hierarchy: the group as a whole dominates any individual who tries to dominate it, using ridicule, gossip, refusal to share, ostracism and, for the persistent, exile or killing.

Richard Lee's fieldwork among the Ju/'hoansi recorded the best-known example. A hunter who brought in a large animal was expected to speak of it dismissively, and his companions mocked the kill as thin and worthless. Lee's informants explained the custom, paraphrased here: a man who comes to think of himself as a chief will one day treat the rest as his servants, so his heart has to be cooled. Sharing rules gave the hunter no special portion. In many groups the owner of the arrow, who was often not the man who shot it, nominally owned the carcass and distributed it.

There was a practical logic under the ethics. In an immediate-return economy nobody can convert a good day into lasting power, because nothing lasts. The only route to dominance is personal: being stronger, more aggressive, more persuasive. The band's weapons, projectile tools that let a small person kill a large one at a distance, made that route dangerous for the would-be chief, since anyone he bullied could end the matter from thirty paces.

Work, diet and what it cost

In Lee's time-allocation study at Dobe in the 1960s, adults spent on average somewhat over two days a week acquiring food, for a diet of around two thousand calories with ample protein, in a semi-desert during a drought year when neighbouring Bantu farmers were on famine relief. Marshall Sahlins built the finding into his argument for what he called the original affluent society: foragers were affluent because they wanted little, and the wanting was kept low by the impossibility of keeping anything.

Later work tempered the picture, since the Dobe figures left out food processing, firewood and water, and perhaps four in ten children did not reach adulthood. The broad finding held. A healthy forager population ate reasonably well for modest work, and the first farmers almost certainly worked harder and ate worse than their foraging grandparents. Part II will take up why they farmed anyway.

What could not happen

Without storable food there is no surplus, and the absences follow from that one fact in a chain. Nothing can be taxed or stolen in quantity, so no specialist can be fed who does not forage, so there are no full-time priests, soldiers, scribes or kings. Nothing durable needs counting, so there is little use for counting and none for writing. Nobody has a reason to stay put, so nobody builds anything larger than a shelter. The band lacked a state because wild food cannot be piled up and guarded, and a society whose wealth cannot be piled up gives no one a foundation to stand above the rest.

The next chapters follow this economy as it spreads across the planet, picks up the bow, the needle and the boat, and begins to trade over long distances. Then, in Part II, a change in climate puts a storable grain within reach of people in the Fertile Crescent, and every constraint in this chapter comes off at once.
